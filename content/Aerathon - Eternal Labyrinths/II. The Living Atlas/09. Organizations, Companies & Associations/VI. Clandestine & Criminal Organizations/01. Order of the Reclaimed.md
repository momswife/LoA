# Order of the Reclaimed

> _“Guard one another’s nights.”_
> — Reclaimed maxim, adapted from a common Selûnite prayer

**Filed Division:** _Living Atlas — Organizations, Companies & Associations_

**Document Class:** _Reconstructed Organization Record_

**Research Status:** _Credible but incomplete_

**Access Designation:** _Public summary; operational locations and contacts withheld_

**Revision State:** _Current assessment_

## I. The Crew Behind the Rumors

The **Order of the Reclaimed**, called the **Pale Vigil** in several intercepted references, is less an order in the institutional sense than a crew that gave itself rules before someone else gave it a gallows. Its members are lycanthropes, vampires, dhampirs, and others whose transformations, appetites, or compulsions may place nearby communities at risk.

They hide the newly afflicted, teach one another control, and take responsibility when one of their own begins to slip. Once a member can be trusted beside frightened people and loaded weapons, the same crew puts that danger to work. They hunt predators, pull prisoners from unjust executions, recover people whom wealth or office has made inconvenient to rescue, and go where a lawful badge has been bought, frightened, or ordered to look away.

The Reclaimed do not promise a cure, claim innocence, or call themselves righteous. They offer discipline, refuge, a second chance, and the blunt expectation that each person will answer for what they do next. Their jobs involve trespass, deception, theft, and sometimes killing. They hold no charter, wear no public badge, and answer to no court after the fact. What keeps them from becoming another armed gang is personal accountability: everyone in the house knows who will take away their weapon, drag them out of a bad fight, or put them down if control finally fails.

> [!note] Filing distinction
> The Order is filed here because its members conceal themselves and take justice into their own hands. Its Selûnite traditions do not grant it religious authority, and a worthy target does not make a vigilante killing lawful.

## II. Founding and Historical Record

Most accounts identify **Ser Santiago Valcázar**, a Brethren lycanthrope and former paladin of Selûne, as the Order’s founder. The title used for him within the fellowship is **First Reclaimed**.

Surviving versions of his history agree on a narrow sequence. Valcázar belonged to a martial Selûnite tradition responsible for protecting travelers after dark. He broke an oath while granting sanctuary to people afflicted by a predatory curse, lost the sacred power carried through that oath, and later contracted lycanthropy himself. The names of the sanctuary, the afflicted community, and the other participants vary or have been deliberately removed from accessible copies.

The distinction Valcázar drew from the event became foundational to the Order: his oath had failed to protect people whom he believed deserved sanctuary, but the breaking of that oath did not prove that Selûne had personally rejected him. This view accords with current scholarship on [[Aerathon - Eternal Labyrinths/I. Annals & Antiquities/01. Fundamentals of Recorded Reality/II. Laws of a Changed World/03. Divine Covenants and Sacred Oaths|Divine Covenants and Sacred Oaths]], which warns that the function or failure of sacred magic does not by itself establish a god’s judgment.

No reliable date for the crew’s first night together has entered the public record. Its history survives less as minutes or charters than as stories repeated at safehouses, rules carried from one job to the next, and the testimony of people who remember who came for them when lawful help did not.

## III. Doctrine of Reclamation

The Order’s central teaching is commonly rendered as:

> **A condition may influence desire. It does not determine character.**

The Reclaimed reject both the claim that dangerous supernatural impulses can be overcome by good intentions alone and the claim that experiencing such impulses makes a person irredeemable. Their discipline concentrates upon what they call **the Stillness**: the interval between impulse and action.

Training attempts to lengthen that interval. Members study the bodily and mental signs that precede a loss of control, including altered breathing, fixation upon scent or territory, sensory narrowing, involuntary movement, predatory thought, and the invention of moral excuses for an immediate appetite. Practice is direct and personal. A vampire may sit beside an open bowl of blood while a friend keeps talking to them. A lycanthrope may hold a partial transformation while answering familiar questions. The point is not serenity. It is remaining reachable when the night turns ugly.

The most frequently reported warning within the Order concerns rationalization. To acknowledge _“I want blood”_ is considered dangerous but honest. To decide that a desired victim deserves death may indicate that appetite has begun manufacturing permission.

The Order does not equate successful meditation with harmlessness. Its methods are intended to make dangerous people more capable of choosing and accepting restraint; they do not remove the danger or absolve past acts.

## IV. The Three Anchors

Instruction within the Order is organized around three linked disciplines.

### The Anchor of Flesh

Members learn the physical course of their condition: triggers, transformation stages, hunger cycles, pain responses, sleep disruption, sensory overload, and other early signs. Ignorance of one’s own condition is treated as a preventable hazard.

### The Anchor of Mind

Members practice identifying an impulse without denying it or building a justification around it. This work includes meditation, truthful self-reporting, and rehearsed responses to known patterns of fixation or compulsion.

### The Anchor of Another

Every full member names two people in the crew who may intervene when that member’s judgment becomes compromised. They learn each other’s tells, hungers, excuses, and worst habits. An anchor may pull their partner from a job, take a weapon, lock away blood or a magical catalyst, sit outside a bolted door until dawn, or call the rest of the house for help. Accounts agree that the promise extends to lethal intervention when no safer protection remains.

This third discipline is central to the Order’s meaning. The Reclaimed do not regard self-command as a solitary achievement. A member who refuses accountable restraint is not considered ready for independent service.

## V. A Crew, Not an Army

The Reclaimed use grand-sounding titles, but available testimony describes practical burdens carried by veterans rather than departments in a hierarchy. The person called Warden of the Road checks the guns, knows the routes, and decides who is steady enough to leave. The Warden of Stillness runs control drills and notices who has stopped sleeping. The Warden of the Veil keeps names off warrants and friends out of interrogation rooms. The Moonkeeper tends the crew’s rites and questions every supposed miracle before anyone builds a mission around it.

Santiago is founder, host, and the person most members follow when a job goes wrong. He is not an unchecked commander. Two Wardens may refuse his call, and he cannot decide alone that someone must die or that the crew should enter a political fight. The limits matter precisely because the Reclaimed work outside ordinary oversight.

Important decisions are reportedly made with whoever is present around the same table. Full members argue, recount what they know, and ask who will still stand behind the choice when the blood has dried. Rank carries experience, not exemption from the Three Anchors or immunity from being told to stay home.

The crew has remained small by design. A larger force would require officers, distant cells, and obedience between people who did not know one another well enough to recognize a lie. The Reclaimed would rather leave a job undone than become an army of supernatural fighters directed by strangers.

## VI. Membership, Care, and Accountability

Estimates place the Order at no more than twenty-five to thirty active members, and not all of them ride on every job. Most are Brethren, with a small number of Beastfolk and members of other peoples or uncommon supernatural lineages. No reliable evidence indicates that ancestry determines who receives a bed, a meal, or a chance to prove control.

New arrivals are called **Those Under Watch**, sometimes for months or years. In practice, this means someone shows them where to sleep, someone else hides their bloody clothes, and at least one veteran stays awake through the first dangerous nights. They receive food, medical care, training, protection, and companionship. Doors may be reinforced and weapons may be locked away, but the newcomer is treated as a frightened or dangerous person—not as a specimen waiting for classification.

The Order does not require flawless control before accepting a person. It requires disclosure and cooperation. A member who loses control and reports the failure may remain under stricter supervision. Concealing such a failure is treated as a graver breach because it disables the accountability upon which the sanctuary depends.

The Reclaimed eat, train, mend gear, and recover together. Pairings on a hunt are shaped as much by trust and old arguments as by ability. A member may be terrifyingly capable in the field and still be ordered to remain at the House because the people who know them best do not like the way they are speaking that night.

## VII. Stillwater House and the Pale Basin

The crew’s home is consistently named **Stillwater House**, though its location is withheld from this filing. Descriptions agree that it occupies a ruined Selûnite waystation or observatory along an abandoned route, with most habitable spaces concealed below the surviving structure.

Stillwater is a refuge before it is a headquarters. Its food stores, medical spaces, training rooms, weapons, and restraints all serve people who may arrive frightened, injured, hunted, or dangerous. Jobs begin there, but so do shared meals, sleepless watches, arguments over control, and the difficult morning after someone nearly lost it.

At its center lies a shallow circular pool called the **Pale Basin**. Testimony claims that supernatural compulsions become quieter when a willing subject enters or meditates beside the basin under moonlight. Hunger, rage, and other impulses remain present; the reported effect is clarity rather than purification. Coerced exposure is said to produce a weaker response.

The cause has not been established. Proposed explanations include a surviving covenant, a divine remnant, an altered relic, or a sacred site through which Selûne exerts limited influence. Each possibility is compatible with some known post-Edict phenomena, but no available evidence distinguishes among them. The Order’s own Moonkeeper reportedly refuses to declare one explanation certain.

## VIII. Selûnite Tradition and the Quiet Edict

The Order’s devotion centers upon Selûne as a guide through change, uncertainty, and the long hours in which formal protection is absent. This emphasis is consistent with established accounts of [[Aerathon - Eternal Labyrinths/I. Annals & Antiquities/01. Fundamentals of Recorded Reality/III. Divinity & The Two Heavens/02. The Dawn Pantheon#Selûne — The Moonmaiden|Selûne]], particularly her associations with wanderers, refugees, moonlit roads, dreams, and lives lived between settled institutions.

The fellowship adapts the common prayer _“Guard my nights”_ into _“Guard one another’s nights.”_ The change turns a request for protection into a mutual obligation.

Accounts of dreams, unexpected moonlight, unusual reflections, and altered behavior near Selûnite relics circulate within the Order. None occurs reliably enough to establish instruction from the goddess. Under the [[The Quiet Edict]], dreams, omens, relic responses, and sacred sites remain possible channels of limited divine influence, but they do not provide automatic certainty about authorship or intent. The Reclaimed therefore treat such events as possible guidance rather than command.

This restraint has not prevented religious criticism. Some Selûnite institutions reportedly regard the Order as heretical for offering sanctuary to predatory afflictions or for separating sacred purpose from inherited oath law. The existence of that dispute does not establish which interpretation, if either, most closely reflects Selûne’s intentions.

## IX. The Jobs They Take

The Reclaimed go after threats that can hide behind fear, money, office, or the assumption that only a monster can fight another monster. Credible stories connect them to predatory supernatural hunts, raids against dangerous cult cells, the removal of afflicted prisoners before summary execution, the recovery of abducted or trafficked victims, and dangerous escorts to sanctuary.

Requests reach the crew through favors, frightened intermediaries, coded marks, and people who know someone once carried out of a locked room. Coin helps keep Stillwater standing, but it does not buy the Reclaimed. Before a job, the available crew conducts **the Weighing**. It is described as an argument around a table, not a tribunal: Is the target truly hurting people? Who benefits if the crew acts? Can ordinary authorities handle it? Have those authorities already been bought? What is the frightened client not saying? If killing begins, who is prepared to own it afterward?

They walk away from ordinary debt collection, private revenge, inheritance disputes, witness suppression, commercial killings, convenient political assassination, and any request based only on a target’s supernatural status. Anyone trying to purchase a private execution has come to the wrong table.

Payment may be accepted, but inability to pay does not bar aid. The House survives on a mixture of well-paid jobs, old investments, quiet benefactors, valuables recovered during raids, and money taken from people the crew has put out of business. Its bookkeeping is reportedly adequate for survival and wholly inadequate for a magistrate.

## X. Known Figures

The same five names recur across otherwise independent accounts, often in stories that sound more like a notorious adventuring crew than the leadership of a hidden institution.

- **Ser Santiago Valcázar**, First Reclaimed, opens the door, makes the hardest calls, and still joins advanced control drills in his lycanthropic form. He is followed because the others trust him, not because an outside authority recognizes his title.
- **Elias Draeven**, Warden of the Road, is the vampire gunslinger who chooses weapons, reads a trail, and teaches frightened recruits that being dangerous is not the same as being strong. His history as a killer is neither concealed within the crew nor treated as settled by later heroics.
- **Orsava Keld**, Warden of Stillness, is the ursine lycanthrope most likely to sit through a newcomer’s first violent night and least likely to accept a comforting lie about what happened during it.
- **Sister Vaelisse Corven**, Moonkeeper, keeps the Pale Basin, preserves the rites, and asks whether the voice in someone’s dream truly belonged to the god whose name they gave it.
- **Taviri Sedge**, Warden of the Veil, finds the road without patrols, the innkeeper who will not ask questions, and the name that survives until morning. The nature of her reported affliction has not been authenticated and is withheld from general summaries.

The Order’s full roster is neither verified nor reproduced. This omission protects possible residents and prevents rumor from placing uninvolved afflicted people under suspicion.

## XI. Symbol, Reputation, and Present Assessment

The Order’s reported sign is a broken silver crescent holding a dark circle within its curve. The darkness is contained but not crossed out. Marks may be concealed beneath a weapon grip, inside a coat, upon an ammunition stamp, on a small coin, or under a doorway. No standard uniform is known.

Most residents of Aerathon have never heard the organization’s name. Monster hunters and occult investigators tell less certain stories of cursed prisoners disappearing before execution, afflicted hunters turning upon more predatory members of their own kind, and moon-marked safehouses empty by daylight.

Those who believe the accounts disagree over whether the Reclaimed are rescuers, heretics, killers, or monsters hunting worse monsters. Each description captures part of the evidence. They shelter people whom other institutions may condemn without distinction, then arm some of those same people and send them into fights chosen by friends around a table. Those they save may call it justice. Authorities they deceive, trespass against, or obstruct are unlikely to agree.

The crew’s small size limits its reach and may also be its principal safeguard. Every Reclaimed knows the people beside them well enough to notice hunger disguised as certainty, vengeance disguised as duty, and fear disguised as a sensible plan. A saying attributed to Valcázar explains why the crew refuses to grow:

> _Thirty dangerous people who know one another can remain a family. Three hundred become a weapon._

Current evidence supports the Order’s existence, name, doctrine, familiar leadership roles, and broad pattern of vigilante work. Its location, complete roster, founding date, secondary safehouses, government contacts, and any direct favor from Selûne remain unverified.
