# Work, Guilds, & Livelihoods

_The work that keeps Aerathon alive rarely fits on a broadcast slate._

**Filed Division:** Office of Civil Life, Trade, and Infrastructure  
**Current Revision:** Late Redfall, 3388 A.D.  
**Classification:** Public Reference

---

## I. The Ordinary Economy

Delving is Aerathon's most visible profession, not its only one. Farms, fisheries, herds, mines, mills, docks, caravans, workshops, kitchens, schools, clinics, temples, archives, theaters, courts, repair crews, and households provide the labor on which every expedition depends.

Much essential work is paid poorly or not described as work at all. Caregiving, food preservation, translation, route memory, mending, water management, and community warning networks can keep a settlement functioning without producing a famous artifact or a public rank.

Occupation is not determined by lineage. Bodies, environments, training, access, inheritance, and personal choice may shape what work is practical, but no Folk is presumed naturally suited to service, violence, craft, scholarship, or care.

## II. How People Learn a Trade

Aerathon has no single path from childhood to profession. Common routes include:

- Household and community teaching.
- Workshop apprenticeship.
- Temple, academy, or civic schooling.
- Guild cadet and affiliate programs.
- House service and sponsored study.
- Military, maritime, caravan, or company training.
- Informal teaching circles built around demonstration and practice.

Louvain is a major center of education, certification, and guild training, while Allemance more broadly exports instruction as one of its recognized services. Other regions maintain their own specializations: navigation and performance in Al'Ar, caravan and sacred study in Arneria, engineering and medicine in Oria, and industrial, financial, and transport expertise in Vinyot.

Formal schooling is influential but not the only source of knowledge. A person who learned weather from deck crews, soil from generations of growers, or machinery on a factory floor may know more about a practical danger than a distant credentialed official. Ministry procedure recognizes that lack of formal education does not invalidate evidence.

## III. Guilds and the Delving Economy

The **Free Guild Accords of 2980 A.D.** formalized the modern guild system. Chartered guilds now recruit, train, equip, sponsor, and discipline delvers while also employing clerks, cooks, healers, artificers, porters, advocates, performers, archivists, and maintenance workers.

MDO-sponsored guilds receive public support and priority access to some relic contracts in exchange for oversight, training obligations, and public education. Private charter guilds finance themselves and enjoy greater independence, though they remain subject to law and relic controls. Guild classifications from C through S affect mission access, prestige, and administrative privilege.

The guild is therefore employer, school, insurer, public symbol, and political actor at once. A good charter can provide training, recovery support, transparent contracts, and a path into dangerous work. A bad arrangement can conceal liability, shift risk downward, or make housing and medical care dependent upon obedience.

## IV. Work in the Age of Spectacle

Broadcast delving transformed attention into a form of economic power. Sponsors finance equipment, travel, lodging, legal services, and presentation. Successful delvers sell more than recovered goods: they sell confidence, style, access, and the expectation of a future victory.

The spectacle economy extends far beyond the person before the Oculus. It supports scry technicians, stage designers, commentators, bookmakers, security staff, costume makers, memory editors, publicists, researchers, and vendors around public viewing sites.

Fame can accelerate a career and distort it. A dramatic but reckless guild may attract investment faster than a careful one. A survivor can become more valuable as a story than as a person. A sponsor's assistance may be generous, controlling, or both. Public rankings record performance under a particular system; they do not measure a worker's full worth.

## V. Regional Patterns

Each Greater Region connects labor to power differently.

### Allemance

Agriculture, brewing, preservation, textiles, artisan work, education, and certification support the region's public abundance. Baronies, guild coalitions, councils, and merchant houses share influence. Reputation and civic standing can matter as much as a formal contract.

### Al'Ar

Ports, ships, windcraft, volcanic glass, elemental reagents, music, performance, and festival work thrive through movement. Careers may follow seasons and audiences rather than a fixed employer. The same freedom can leave workers dependent on personal reputation and favorable passage.

### Arneria

Caravan provisioning, route keeping, sacred craft, glass, spice, alchemy, and pilgrimage support dispersed communities. Labor cannot be separated from water, distance, temple authority, and the safety of controlled routes.

### Oria

Mines, machines, infrastructure, cold-weather transport, medicine, and alchemy are commonly organized through the Houses. Skilled work carries honor, but House loyalty and restricted supply chains can limit movement between patrons.

### Vinyot

Canals, ports, factories, finance, arbitration, textiles, glasswork, spell-paper, and relic processing make labor intensely contractual. Companies may control both employment and housing. Organized workers, local councils, and mutual-aid networks contest that concentration of power.

## VI. Safety, Compensation, and Refusal

Dangerous work is not limited to Labyrinths. Mines collapse, workshops contaminate, ships founder, caravans vanish, scry equipment overloads, and factories expose workers to heat, noise, pressure, and magic.

Within its jurisdiction, the Ministry's Delver Health and Recovery Office coordinates rehabilitation, curse monitoring, anomaly screening, psychological care, reintegration, and accommodations. The Office of Compensation and Survivor Claims reviews injury, death, equipment, rescue, liability, and civilian-damage claims. These protections are important but do not form a universal civilian welfare system.

Workers still depend heavily on regional law, guild charter, company policy, House custom, temple care, family resources, and community solidarity. A contract does not make coercion acceptable. The right to understand a risk, request accommodation, report harm, and refuse work remains central to any credible claim of free labor.
