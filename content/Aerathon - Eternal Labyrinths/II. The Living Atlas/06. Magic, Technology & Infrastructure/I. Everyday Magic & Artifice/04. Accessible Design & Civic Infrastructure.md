# Accessible Design & Civic Infrastructure

_A route is accessible only when it leads to a destination a person can actually use._

**Filed Division:** Office of Civil Life, Trade, and Infrastructure  
**Consulting Division:** Delver Health and Recovery Office  
**Current Revision:** Late Redfall, 3388 A.D.  
**Classification:** Public Reference

---

## I. More Than an Entrance

Aerathon's people differ in size, reach, balance, senses, movement, atmosphere, moisture, temperature, light, and forms of communication. Injury, illness, age, magical exposure, and fatigue add further variation within every Folk.

Accessible design does not ask whether a theoretical person can enter a building. It asks whether the actual user can reach the site, enter it, move through it, communicate, work or participate, use water and sanitation, rest, evacuate, and return home.

Centaur advocates often call this the **whole destination** standard. A broad street is of little use if the clinic ends in a narrow turning room. A ramp into a school does not help if its desks, toilets, alarms, and lesson materials remain unusable.

## II. Routes and Movement

Useful public routes account for width, turning space, surface, slope, overhead clearance, rest intervals, crowding, water depth, landing space, and safe separation from vehicles or machinery.

Different solutions may coexist:

- Ramps, broad gates, low-gradient streets, lifts, and stable platforms.
- Railings, tactile markers, contrasting edges, and predictable crossings.
- Flight approaches and landing places that do not make stairs the only secondary route.
- Flooded channels, water chairs, support cradles, and transfer spaces for aquatic residents and visitors.
- Low counters, reachable controls, small-scale refuge routes, and protection from crowd crush.
- Quiet waiting areas and routes sheltered from intense sound, glare, smoke, or resonance.

Meran water access illustrates why a token feature is not enough. A usable **Guest Current** must consider depth, salinity, temperature, privacy, entry, and exit. A decorative pool is not transportation, sanitation, or a safe place to rest.

## III. Prosthetics and Personal Equipment

Aerathic prosthetics range from ordinary crafted supports to mechanical limbs, enchanted mobility harnesses, water chairs, sensory aids, flight supports, and aetheric devices. Oric engineering, Vinyot production, the Iron-Bough Syndicate, medical specialists, and community craftspeople all contribute to the field.

No device makes its user “complete,” and no user owes admiration for wearing one. Fit, comfort, repair, control, appearance, cost, and the right to remove or refuse a device belong to the person using it.

Personal equipment also requires public support. A lift that cannot carry a chair, a checkpoint that confiscates a necessary focus, or an employer who forbids maintenance breaks turns the environment—not the device—into the immediate barrier.

Magical prosthetics require the same cautions as other artifice: understandable controls, safe shutdown, documented materials, repair access, and monitoring for unexpected resonance. An upgrade should never be performed merely to improve a worker's output without their informed consent.

## IV. Communication and Sensory Access

Public information should not depend on one sense or one language. Important notices are strongest when they combine several methods: speech, text, sign, image, tactile marking, light, vibration, personal guidance, and clear physical demonstration.

Volume is not clarity. Brighter light is not always safer. Magical translation can assist communication without replacing patience, context, or the speaker's right to their own language.

Elven low-resonance rooms, dampening veils, and quiet spaces demonstrate that withdrawal can be a designed form of participation rather than exclusion. A person may need to step out of a noisy council and still receive the record, vote, lesson, or service that follows.

Scry broadcasts and public displays require captions or written summaries, audible or signed explanation, warnings before intense effects, and alternatives for people who cannot remain in a crowded viewing area.

## V. Utilities and Public Works

Infrastructure includes roads, bridges, canals, lifts, rail, lighting, water, drainage, heat, ventilation, waste handling, warning systems, and public communication. Magic and machinery often share the same structure.

An accessible system must remain usable during maintenance and partial failure. A magically powered lift needs a rescue procedure. A heated shelter needs ventilation and a nonverbal alarm. A canal district needs dry routes as well as wet ones. A public square designed for flyers still needs ground access and protected landing space.

Maintenance is an accessibility practice. Broken paving, drifting calibration, blocked transfers, missing signs, and delayed replacement parts can erase the benefit of a thoughtful design more quickly than law can recognize the failure.

## VI. Emergency Planning

Emergencies reveal whose needs were treated as optional. Evacuation plans should account for people who cannot use the fastest route, hear a bell, read a scry display, fly in bad air, leave water safely, tolerate a crowded shelter, or travel without medication and equipment.

Good planning identifies assistance without assigning ownership of one person to another. It provides redundant alerts, accessible shelters, power or charging where required, privacy for care, suitable food and environment, and reunification procedures for households separated by the evacuation.

The Ministry's Delver Health and Recovery Office coordinates flight, mobility, and sensory accommodations within its jurisdiction. Local governments, Houses, guilds, companies, temples, and communities remain responsible for the many residents outside that specialized system.

## VII. Consultation and Accountability

Standards are strongest when designed with the people who use them. Consultation must include varied bodies and needs rather than selecting one visible representative for an entire Folk.

An accessibility feature should be tested under ordinary crowd, weather, work, and emergency conditions. Complaints need an understandable route, repairs need deadlines, and a temporary closure needs a genuine alternative.

The question is not whether a builder intended inclusion. The question is whether people can participate with safety, dignity, privacy, and choice.
