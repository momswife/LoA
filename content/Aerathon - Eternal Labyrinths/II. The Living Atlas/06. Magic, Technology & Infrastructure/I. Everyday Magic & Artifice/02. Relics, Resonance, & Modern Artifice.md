# Relics, Resonance, & Modern Artifice

_Aerathon's modern wonders are built from discovery, craft, danger, and decisions about who is allowed to benefit._

**Filed Division:** Office of Civil Life, Trade, and Infrastructure  
**Consulting Division:** Arcane and Anomalous Research Directorate  
**Current Revision:** Late Redfall, 3388 A.D.  
**Classification:** Public Reference

---

## I. Relic and Invention

A **relic** is recovered, not merely manufactured. It comes from a Labyrinth, ruin, anomalous event, or other context in which origin and behavior may be uncertain. A piece of modern **artifice** is deliberately designed, assembled, and tested, even when its makers use lessons or materials derived from relic study.

The difference protects both history and safety. Calling an invention a relic can disguise its maker's responsibility. Calling a relic an ordinary tool can hide an unknown danger.

Modern Aerathon depends on a productive tension between the two. Relics reveal materials, effects, and structures that contemporary artificers could not have imagined. Artificers translate some of those discoveries into repeatable devices that can be repaired, regulated, taught, and—at least in principle—understood.

## II. Resonance

Many magical systems operate through **resonance**: a patterned response between material, rune, memory, emotion, place, or magical field. Resonance allows an Oculus to transmit, a relay to carry a signal, an anchor to stabilize a reading, or a crafted device to repeat a bounded effect.

Resonance is not one invisible fuel shared by every object. Two devices may respond to entirely different conditions. A material that carries a scry signal safely might store dangerous memory, reject a foreign rune, or become unstable near Labyrinth influence.

For that reason, compatibility is tested rather than assumed. Identical-looking components are not freely interchangeable when their provenance, prior exposure, or attunement differs.

## III. From Recovery to Public Use

The path from a recovered object to an everyday technology is deliberately slow:

1. **Recovery and containment** establish immediate safety and lawful custody.
2. **Reporting and classification** document where the object was found, what it affected, and what remains unknown.
3. **Observation and testing** identify repeatable behavior without assuming that a successful activation is safe.
4. **Design translation** separates a useful principle from the original object's unstable or inaccessible form.
5. **Prototype and review** test the new device under controlled conditions and assign responsibility for failure.
6. **Manufacture and maintenance** determine whether the design can be produced, repaired, taught, and supplied without concealing its costs.

Not every relic should complete this path. Some remain evidence, sacred property, active hazards, or objects whose extraction already caused unacceptable harm.

## IV. The Guilds of Artifice

The **Iron-Bough Syndicate** represents mechanized delving and heavy engineering. Its drills, elevators, automatons, containment equipment, mechanical prosthetics, and industrial systems turn recovered material into infrastructure at great scale. Supporters credit it with making dangerous work survivable and large projects possible. Critics point to extraction, ecological damage, worker risk, and the practice of reducing irreplaceable relics to components.

The **Emberlight Union** works through illumination, performance, portable magitech, simulations, theaters, festivals, relic refurbishment, and public art. Its inventions make magic visible and approachable. Its patents and presentation culture also raise questions about who owns a technique learned from shared or ancient material.

These guilds are prominent, not exclusive. Oric Houses maintain engineering and alchemical traditions; Vinyot factories mass-produce components; Louvain trains and certifies specialists; Al'Ari craftspeople work with wind and elemental materials; Arnerian makers join sacred law, glass, metal, spice, and alchemy in ways foreign inspectors may misunderstand.

## V. Ownership, Provenance, and Regulation

Relic ownership may involve the discoverer, land or route authority, sponsoring guild, harmed community, religious claimant, prior keeper, and the Ministry. Possession at the moment of recovery settles none of these claims by itself.

Provenance records are not decorative paperwork. They preserve location, custody, exposure, testing, modification, and transfer. Without them, an appraiser cannot reliably distinguish a stable component from a contaminated one or a lawful sale from concealed theft.

Public safety rules require prompt reporting and appropriate containment. Exact procedures depend on classification and jurisdiction. The current field standard is maintained in [[Aerathon - Eternal Labyrinths/II. The Living Atlas/07. Delving, Guilds & the Spectacle/II. Field Operations & Safety/03. Relic Handling & Regulation|Relic Handling & Regulation]].

Regulation does not guarantee justice. A technically complete chain of custody may still begin with coercive excavation or exclude the community that preserved the object. The Archive records lawful status and disputed legitimacy separately when necessary.

## VI. Industry and Unequal Benefit

Relic-derived technology supports lifts, machines, prosthetics, containment, civic lighting, scrying, transport, medicine, and entertainment. Its benefits are real. So are the inequalities around them.

Wealthy districts receive stable installations and routine maintenance. Remote settlements may receive obsolete devices without replacement parts. Workers can be exposed during extraction and fabrication while consumers encounter only the polished result. A guild may advertise public service while controlling the patent, fuel, technician, and contract required to keep that service operating.

The best public artifice is not merely impressive. It has a documented purpose, understandable controls, trained maintainers, safe shutdown, accessible use, available repair, known disposal, and an honest account of the people and materials that made it possible.
