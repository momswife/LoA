# House, Service & Repair

In much of Oria, House affiliation is more immediate than regional identity. A House is a lodge-city and governing community, not a bloodline. It organizes maintenance, defense, provision, testimony, care, and public service while competing with other Houses for prestige and influence.

## I. Ways of Belonging

People may identify themselves through birthplace, current residence, service, craft, household, or House connection. These relationships need not coincide. A person can move between Houses, retain affection for a birthplace, or serve a House without treating its steward family as kin.

Movement carries obligations because House infrastructure is built around mutual survival. Entry, residence, training, equipment, winter provision, and protection may each create responsibilities. Those responsibilities should be stated rather than inferred from ancestry or mere presence.

## II. Service and Competence

Skilled work carries public honor when it keeps people, structures, and routes alive. Repair, medicine, alchemy, engineering, mining, cold-weather transport, preparation, and emergency response all contribute to House standing. Competence is witnessed through consequences: a repaired wall holds, supplies arrive, a patient recovers, or an evacuation succeeds.

This respect for competence does not make every Oric a craftsperson or soldier. It also does not justify denying rest, accommodation, refusal, or recognition to the people whose labor is less visible. A culture of service remains answerable to those performing it.

## III. Rivalry and Shared Danger

Houses compete in martial skill, craftsmanship, scholarship, endurance, and delving. Rivalry is expected to stop when failure threatens more than one jurisdiction. Shared defense, passage, and infrastructure can bring the Houses into a Housemeet, where recognized decisions create temporary or issue-specific obligations.

The ideal is not permanent harmony. It is reliable cooperation under pressure. Local accounts differ on how fairly prestige is awarded, how easily a person can change affiliation, and whether established Houses recognize expertise developed beyond their institutions.

## IV. Authority Is Not Ancestry

Steward families and civic offices may hold a House Seat, but they do not own the House as hereditary property. Recognition depends upon the community, its councils, and the specialists whose cooperation makes authority real. Formal political details belong in the regional and subregional government dossiers.

Cultural records should therefore describe how service and belonging are practiced without converting House affiliation into lineage, personality, or unquestioning obedience. Settlement-specific and House-specific evidence remains more authoritative than an assumed Oric norm.
