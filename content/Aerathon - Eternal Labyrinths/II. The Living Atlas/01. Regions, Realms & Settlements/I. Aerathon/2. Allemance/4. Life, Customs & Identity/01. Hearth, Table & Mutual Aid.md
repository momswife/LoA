# Hearth, Table & Mutual Aid

Across Allemance, hearth, table, neighborhood, guild, and remembered place often carry more immediate meaning than a single regional identity. Shared meals and provision recur in public memory because feeding and sheltering people create relationships, but the authority of those relationships depends upon local witnesses and law.

## I. Provision as Responsibility

The Table Oath expresses a recurring Allemagnian principle: provision creates responsibility. Its legal, sacred, or personal force is not uniform. A formal oath before recognized witnesses differs from an ordinary meal, and hospitality never gives a host ownership of a guest's choices, worship, information, or future labor.

Feastcraft, communal ovens, stored grain, neighborhood kitchens, and emergency shelter all connect food with civic survival. These practices appear in cities, baronies, roadside communities, and displaced households in different forms. They should not be compressed into a claim that every Allemagnian household follows one etiquette.

## II. Hearth, Household & Memory

Households preserve belonging through residence, kinship, adoption, apprenticeship, work, and mutual care. The hearth can be domestic, institutional, or temporary: a family kitchen, guild dormitory, refuge house, caravan assembly, or neighborhood shelter may all become places where obligations are remembered.

Older Hearth-Stone traditions remain visible wherever shelter is treated as a mutual promise during danger. Their survival does not establish a single unbroken custom, and local communities disagree about who may invoke hospitality, how long it lasts, and what restitution follows abuse.

## III. Regional Differences

Louvain's academies, guilds, markets, and dense neighborhoods sustain forms of public provision unlike those of river baronies or border settlements. Glasrún places greater weight on local stewardship, remembered precedent, and hearth-scale authority. Displaced communities may carry household customs across several jurisdictions while adapting them to new neighbors and material conditions.

The regional pattern is therefore one of recurring concern with provision and remembered obligation, not cultural uniformity. Settlement and subregional records govern the details.

## IV. Mutual Aid & Uneven Access

Neighborhood kitchens, repair gatherings, warning networks, local healers, school circles, and shelter agreements fill needs that crown, barony, guild, temple, or Ministry services do not always meet. These networks can be generous without being inexhaustible. Access still varies by wealth, remoteness, citizenship, disability, and political standing.

Mutual aid is strongest when the people receiving care retain consent and standing within the relationship. A gift that creates unpayable debt, coerced loyalty, or silence about harm contradicts the public ethic that provision should answer need.
