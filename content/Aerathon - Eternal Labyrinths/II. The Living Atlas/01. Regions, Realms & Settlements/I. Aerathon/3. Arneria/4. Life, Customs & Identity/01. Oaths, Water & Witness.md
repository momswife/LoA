# Oaths, Water & Witness

Arnerian public life is often described through oaths, discipline, water, and responsible use of scarce resources. Those themes cross several jurisdictions, but their meaning changes sharply between Causeway institutions, Beylik authority, Bat'yani communities, caravan routes, and other local settlements.

## I. Oaths and Their Witnesses

An oath gains authority from the people, office, ritual, and jurisdiction that witness it. A Causeway declaration filed before officials is not equivalent to a household promise, village settlement, pilgrimage vow, or agreement made at a water source. Travelers should not assume that one form can compel recognition from another community.

Formal authorities value records, schedules, permits, and disciplined performance. Local communities may place greater confidence in remembered conduct and witnesses who remain answerable to the people affected. Both systems can preserve responsibility; both can also be used to exclude testimony they do not recognize.

## II. Water and Shared Survival

Water access shapes hospitality, movement, labor, and public safety. Wells, cisterns, caravan stores, forest waterways, and controlled routes require different forms of care. Waste, contamination, concealment of danger, and refusal of emergency access can harm people well beyond the immediate user.

The resulting customs are practical before they are symbolic. People who maintain a source, carry supplies, monitor seasonal change, or guide others through dangerous conditions may hold authority grounded in demonstrated responsibility. That authority should not be mistaken for ownership of every person who depends upon the resource.

## III. Causeway and Bat'yan Life

Causeway communities commonly organize daily life around schedules, checkpoints, public ceremony, trade movement, and the needs of controlled infrastructure. In Bat'yan, barangay and village relationships, ecological knowledge, seasonal conditions, shared meals, communal labor, and locally maintained sacred boundaries carry greater weight.

These patterns are not two biological populations or complete opposites. People travel, intermarry, serve, trade, worship, and build households across the divide. The distinction describes institutions and lived conditions, not inherited temperament.

## IV. Discipline, Consent & Public Responsibility

Discipline is respected when it makes danger legible and distributes responsibility. It becomes coercive when obedience is treated as proof of belonging or when distant authority overrides the people bearing the cost. Likewise, local autonomy does not excuse concealed harm or the exclusion of affected neighbors.

Current cultural filing should therefore identify the actual witnesses, resource system, and community involved. “Arnerian custom” is too broad when the evidence belongs specifically to a checkpoint, barangay, temple, caravan, household, or settlement.
