# Currency, Credit, & Exchange

_How Aerathon decides what something is worth—and whether a promise will hold._

**Filed Division:** Office of Civil Life, Trade, and Infrastructure  
**Current Revision:** Late Redfall, 3388 A.D.  
**Classification:** Public Reference

---

## I. No Single Measure of Worth

Coin is accepted across much of Aerathon, but coin is only one part of exchange. A traveler may pay a market stall in minted metal, secure passage through a written contract, receive lodging through a guild seal, and repay a village with labor or a future favor—all on the same journey.

The Living Atlas therefore does not describe one currency as universally dominant. Local mints, merchant weights, House guarantees, temple records, company accounts, and guild-backed notes overlap. What matters is not only the object offered, but who recognizes it and what authority stands behind it.

Major markets usually employ weighers, money changers, appraisers, or clerks. Smaller communities often rely on long familiarity: a shopkeeper knows which seal is sound, which family settles its debts, and which bright coin has been shaved at the edge.

## II. Coin and Account

Precious-metal coin remains the easiest form of payment to carry between jurisdictions. Gold-standard values are also used for large comparisons, including guild founding funds, even when the actual deposit consists of several accepted forms of wealth.

Coins are not interchangeable merely because they shine. Their value may depend on metal content, mint, age, condition, and the willingness of a local authority to redeem them. Merchants traveling far from home commonly keep mixed purses or convert wealth at established ports and capitals.

One of the best-known named currencies is Louvain's **Crownflame**, a soft-gold coin said to glow faintly in moonlight. Its familiarity reflects Louvain's influence in certification, relic appraisal, education, and international administration; it does not make every rural seller or foreign council answerable to Louvain's price.

Daily purchases may also be recorded on account. An inn, workshop, household supplier, or neighborhood market can extend modest credit to known customers. Larger accounts require clearer witnesses: ledgers, seals, contract clerks, collateral, or a recognized sponsor.

## III. Credit, Contracts, and Sponsorship

Credit is organized trust. In some places that trust rests on a family name or personal history. Elsewhere it rests on a company, House, temple, guild, or government office willing to enforce the agreement.

Vinyot makes the contract especially visible. Companies and boards may control employment, housing, insurance, transport, and market access within the same district. This can make credit efficient while also making debt difficult to escape: losing one's employer may mean losing one's room, protection, and recognized account at once.

Oric trade is more often arranged House by House. No outside buyer should assume that an agreement with one House grants access to another's mines, workshops, healers, escorts, or supply chain. The separation is deliberate and helps prevent any foreign power from controlling the whole northern economy.

Guild sponsorship operates across borders. Sponsors may provide equipment, lodging, transport, legal help, or broadcast access in return for service, publicity, future relic shares, or contractual loyalty. A famous seal can open a door more quickly than a heavy purse. It can also make a worker dependent upon the institution whose mark they carry.

## IV. Obligation, Reputation, and Witness

Several Aerathic economies treat reputation as practical tender without pretending it is the same as coin.

In Allemance, reliable hospitality, fulfilled obligations, and local standing may carry more weight than distant wealth. In Al'Ar, reputation and **Witness Marks** can validate that a person arrived, performed, served, or kept a promise. Among Verglass communities, remembered obligation may matter where ordinary coin has little meaning.

These systems are not primitive barter. They are records of relationship. A promise witnessed by the right community may be safer than an unfamiliar note; it may also be impossible to transfer to a stranger. Obligation works best where all parties understand what is owed, when it ends, and whether it can be inherited or sold.

The Ministry advises visitors never to assume that hospitality creates a debt, that a gift permits a claim on the recipient, or that an inherited feud is a lawful account. Local custom must still yield to consent and applicable law.

## V. Relics as Wealth

Relics complicate every ordinary theory of price. They may be art, evidence, weapon, sacred object, industrial component, public hazard, or several of these at once. Their value can rise after authentication and collapse after contamination, a disputed provenance, or a change in regulation.

Relic finders do not receive an unrestricted right to sell what they carry out of a Labyrinth. Reporting, containment, classification, and ownership review may come first. Guild contracts and sponsorship agreements can further divide a relic's eventual proceeds between delvers, institutions, claimants, and damaged communities.

For handling requirements, consult [[Aerathon - Eternal Labyrinths/II. The Living Atlas/07. Delving, Guilds & the Spectacle/II. Field Operations & Safety/Relic Handling & Regulation|Relic Handling & Regulation]].

## VI. What Travelers Should Carry

A prudent traveler carries several ways to prove value:

- Accepted coin in more than one denomination.
- A written identity or guild record where such documents are recognized.
- Copies of major contracts rather than the only original.
- The name of a local sponsor, witness, or receiving house.
- A reserve that is not controlled by an employer or travel company.
- A clear understanding of whether food, lodging, healing, tolls, and return passage are included.

No seal defeats hunger, and no amount of coin guarantees that a flooded road, closed border, or frightened village has goods to sell. Wealth is most reliable when it is paired with time, local knowledge, and relationships that were not created under duress.
