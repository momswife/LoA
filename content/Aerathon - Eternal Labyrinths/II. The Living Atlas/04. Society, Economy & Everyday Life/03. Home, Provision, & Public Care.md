# Home, Provision, & Public Care

_A home is not merely a roof. It is access to food, rest, privacy, safety, learning, care, and a way back inside._

**Filed Division:** Office of Civil Life, Trade, and Infrastructure  
**Current Revision:** Late Redfall, 3388 A.D.  
**Classification:** Public Reference

---

## I. Food and Daily Provision

Aerathon's tables begin with geography. Allemance supplies grain, ale, preserved food, and produce to markets far beyond its borders. Vinyot's waterways move food at great volume but make its dense cities dependent on uninterrupted routes. Al'Ar imports much of its food and timber. Oria's cities and Houses balance northern stores with imported provisions. Arnerian communities organize survival around caravan supplies, fragile soils, water access, and carefully maintained routes.

Most households obtain food through some mixture of markets, direct production, wages, household stores, shared kitchens, temple or civic provision, and relationships with growers or traders. Inns and street vendors matter to workers who cannot cook where they sleep. Granaries, bakeries, preserved goods, and communal ovens matter whenever harvest and travel seasons fall out of step.

Food is also culture and consent. Hospitality may carry sacred, familial, or political meaning, but no offering creates a right to pressure a guest to eat, drink, repay, worship, or disclose information. Dietary need cannot be guessed from a person's Folk. Sapient communities differ in digestion, environment, ritual, allergy, and personal choice.

Late Redfall is a season of accounting. Stores are inspected, routes are watched, winter agreements are tested, and shortages become political before they become visible at every table.

## II. Housing and Belonging

Housing ranges from farmsteads, village homes, roadside shelters, temple quarters, and caravan camps to urban apartments, guild dormitories, House compounds, company districts, canal dwellings, and water-filled Meran residences.

The person who owns a building may not be the person who governs daily life inside it. Families, employers, Houses, temples, councils, landlords, guilds, and neighborhood circles all create different rules of residence. In some Vinyot districts, a company may own housing while also controlling wages, transport, and security. Guild lodging can protect a new worker while binding shelter to continued service.

Older Drift traditions preserve another model. Hearth-Stone Compacts treated shared shelter as a mutual promise rather than proof of common ancestry or worship. Their influence remains visible in hostels, refuge houses, caravan assemblies, and neighborhoods where belonging is measured by who will make room during danger.

Housing quality is inseparable from access. A nominal room is not usable if its entrance, water, air, sound, light, temperature, furniture, or evacuation route excludes its resident. Accessible Design & Civic Infrastructure|Accessible Design & Civic Infrastructure]].

## III. Education

Children and adults learn through households, schools, temples, academies, guilds, apprenticeships, military or maritime service, workshops, and informal teaching circles. The balance varies by region and wealth.

Louvain is internationally known for formal education, delver certification, and specialized scholarship. Guild charters also require access to training, and MDO-sponsored guilds owe a measure of public education or civic service. Emberlight's Glowline Initiative provides applied magitech teaching to impoverished youth, demonstrating how a guild program can widen access while also shaping the labor force that later enters its industry.

Education includes far more than literacy. Route knowledge, weather reading, farming, first aid, hand sign, trade notation, craft, local law, oral history, religious practice, and the safe use of magic may be taught outside any academy. Common itself grew from contact among caravans, displaced communities, hand sign, legal forms, and trade speech rather than from a single decree.

A certificate can help strangers trust one another. It should not be used to erase expertise held by people whose learning was communal, practical, or inaccessible to official examination.

## IV. Medicine and Healing

Care may come from physicians, herbalists, alchemists, clerics, surgeons, community healers, rehabilitation workers, artificers, or specialists familiar with a particular body or magical exposure. None is sufficient for every injury.

Magical healing can close wounds and still leave pain, disability, trauma, contamination, debt, or a need for long recovery. Regrowth is neither effortless nor guaranteed. Curses and Labyrinth anomalies may require observation long after a person appears well.

Oria is widely respected for medical expertise and alchemical treatment. The MDO's Delver Health and Recovery Office provides specialized support for delvers and survivors, including curse monitoring, psychological care, reintegration, and mobility or sensory accommodations. Temple and community care remain essential where Ministry services do not reach or do not apply.

Medical practice must account for enormous bodily variation without turning Folk classification into destiny. Consent is required for examination, samples, magical observation, experimental treatment, and the reuse of a patient's experience as research or spectacle. Receiving care should not create an obligation to become study material.

## V. Mutual Aid and Uneven Access

Formal services are only one layer of public care. Neighborhood kitchens, donor cooperatives, repair gatherings, roadside shelters, school circles, warning networks, community clinics, and quiet rooms often fill the distance between law and lived need.

These systems can be generous without being inexhaustible. Communities with the greatest need may have the least coin, staff, stable magic, or political leverage. Remote settlements face travel delays. Company districts may make care dependent on employment. Famous delvers can receive expensive treatment while civilian workers harmed by the same event struggle to prove a claim.

The clearest measure of provision is not whether a service exists somewhere on a map. It is whether the person who needs it can reach it, enter it, understand it, afford it, consent to it, and return home afterward.
