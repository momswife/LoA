# **The Crown Concord**

### _The Settlement That Ended the War of Seven Crowns by Ranking the Unresolved_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Treaty and Settlement Archive  
**Supporting Offices:** Dynastic Records Desk, Comparative Sovereignty Commission, Crown-Era Miracle Review  
**Primary Compiler:** Magistrate Talar Shellmark, Comparative Legal Traditions  
**Contributing Scholars:** Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Priest-Analyst Thera Daan, Professor Arav Dhal-Senn  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Inter-Crown Settlement / Grade-II Politically Curated Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** surviving concord rolls, enthronement registers, marriage compacts, miracle audits, provincial copies, civic ledgers, and later Faith Court commentary  
**Historical Range:** Ratified 420 A.D., during [[The Age of Crowns|the Age of Crowns]]

## **I. The Settlement**

The **Crown Concord** was ratified in **420 A.D.** after the exhaustion of [[The War of Seven Crowns]].

The war had failed to establish one universally legitimate monarch. The Concord did not solve that failure. It converted competing sacred claims into a ranked diplomatic order.

At its summit stood the Three High Thrones:

- the [[The Bell-Crown of Louvain|Bell-Crown of Louvain]];
- the [[The Stone Throne of Dhalraen|Stone Throne of Dhalraen]];
- and the [[The Sun Court of Vaelcour|Sun Court of Vaelcour]].

Other realms received lesser standing according to the evidence their rulers could present and the recognition stronger crowns were willing to grant.

The Concord reduced open warfare.

It made legitimacy a permanent international proceeding.

---

## **II. The Five Ranks**

Surviving records recognize five broad stations:

1. **High Throne** — a sovereign power whose sacred authority could judge or recognize other crowns.
2. **Anointed Crown** — a recognized monarchy possessing substantial divine or genealogical proof but lacking equal standing with a High Throne.
3. **Petitionary Crown** — a ruler whose claim required continuing review, sponsorship, or renewed evidence.
4. **Stewarded Seat** — a territory permitted local rule under the supervision of a higher-ranked crown.
5. **Hollow Crown** — a title judged ritually or genealogically defective, tolerated temporarily but denied full sacred legitimacy.

These categories were not neutral descriptions. Rank affected marriage, trade, border recognition, diplomatic precedence, and whether intervention in a succession dispute could be called conquest or correction.

To be declared Hollow was often more dangerous than military defeat.

It authorized neighbors to treat a realm’s sovereignty as an unresolved error.

---

## **III. Proof of Rule**

The Concord formalized the evidence by which sacred monarchy was assessed:

- witnessed miracles;
- genealogical continuity;
- relic custody;
- ritual fitness;
- resonance between ruler, territory, and inherited office;
- recognition by an established throne;
- and the ability to maintain public sacred obligations.

No category was applied consistently.

Louvain emphasized witness and civic record. Dhalraen privileged endurance, oath, and material continuity. Vaelcour elevated illumination and blood. Smaller crowns adapted claims to whichever High Throne offered the most favorable hearing.

The Concord therefore standardized the requirement for proof without standardizing what proof meant.

---

## **IV. Marriage, Blood, and Audit**

Dynastic marriage became an instrument of compliance.

Unions could raise a Petitionary Crown, stabilize a disputed succession, or subordinate a lesser realm to a High Throne. Children were examined for signs of inherited resonance. Household histories became matters of international law.

This system encouraged increasingly invasive genealogical audit.

Families concealed adoptees, altered birth records, purchased witnesses, and destroyed evidence of kinships that complicated sacred blood claims. Vaelcour’s doctrines of illumination strongly influenced later Faith Density law, but all three High Thrones participated in making ancestry politically measurable.

The Concord ended battles over the Chamber of Measures.

It distributed smaller chambers into every royal household.

---

## **V. Peace and Its Price**

The Concord succeeded in its narrow purpose. Large-scale war among the most powerful crowns became less frequent. Disputes moved into marriage negotiation, ritual hearing, relic arbitration, and proxy conflict.

For populations beneath the crowns, the distinction was uneven.

A peaceful succession audit could erase a household from law. A ritual deposition could redirect taxes, land, and military service without a siege. Lesser realms bore the cost of maintaining proofs designed by stronger states.

The settlement made theology into international law.

It also created forums through which weaker crowns sometimes resisted annexation. A Petitionary Crown with a recognized hearing could delay a stronger neighbor, preserve evidence, and attract competing sponsors.

Hierarchy constrained violence while creating new forms of it.

---

## **VI. From Crown Concord to Binding Concord**

Over the following centuries, [[The Faith Courts|Faith Courts]] increasingly administered the Concord’s audits.

Questions once limited to rulers expanded toward clergy, households, professions, and ordinary believers. Miracle verification became licensing. Dynastic registries became models for the [[The Registry of Souls|Registry of Souls]]. Sacred obligations once negotiated among crowns became claims upon populations.

In **1091 A.D.**, the remaining High Thrones and Faith Courts ratified [[The Binding Concord]].

The later settlement did not reject the Crown Concord.

It extended its logic beyond the palace.

---

## **VII. Ministry Conclusions**

1. The Crown Concord ended the War of Seven Crowns by ranking rival claims rather than identifying one rightful monarch.
2. The Three High Thrones gained authority to shape the legitimacy of lesser realms.
3. Its five ranks affected sovereignty, marriage, trade, and lawful intervention.
4. It standardized the demand for sacred proof while leaving proof politically contested.
5. The Concord reduced open warfare and expanded genealogical, ritual, and administrative coercion.
6. It also gave some lesser realms a recognized forum from which to resist immediate annexation.
7. The Binding Concord later extended Crown-Era audit into imperial government.
