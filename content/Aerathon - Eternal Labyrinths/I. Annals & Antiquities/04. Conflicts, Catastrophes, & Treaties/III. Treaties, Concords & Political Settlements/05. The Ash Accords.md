# The Ash Accords

### _The Late Ember Restrictions Negotiated While the Catastrophe Was Still Burning_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Treaty and Settlement Archive  
**Supporting Offices:** Environmental Harm Archive, Craft Lineage Commission, Fold Phenomena Desk  
**Primary Compiler:** Researcher Falyn Onarra, Cascading Rupture Studies  
**Contributing Scholars:** Professor Arav Dhal-Senn, Magistrate Talar Shellmark, Archivist Yelna Stonevoice  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Emergency Technical Settlement / Grade-I Abundant Compliance Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** signed restrictions, inspection reports, exemption petitions, concealed workshop surveys, disaster-aid ledgers, and violation proceedings  
**Historical Range:** Negotiated 2594 A.D., during the [[The Ignition Catastrophes and Cinder Years|Cinder Years]]

## I. The Emergency Settlement

The **Ash Accords** were negotiated in **2594 A.D.** by surviving members of the [[The Concord of Flame|Concord of Flame]] and associated states.

By then, the Ignition Catastrophes had persisted for decades. Aether storms, engine autonomy, failed harvest systems, transmutation collapse, and expanding Folds had made isolated regulation ineffective.

The delegates did not meet after the crisis.

They met inside it.

Several routes to the assembly failed during negotiation. Two delegations arrived without the governments that had appointed them. At least one signatory ratified restrictions while its own prohibited engines remained necessary to contain a neighboring disaster.

The Accords were therefore both law and triage.

---

## II. Principal Restrictions

The surviving core text restricts three broad classes of work.

### A. High-Order Elemental Work

Projects capable of altering regional heat, weather, water, pressure, or ground resonance required multi-state notice, independent review, and accessible failure procedures.

### B. Autonomous Spell Engines

Engines able to revise their own instructions, recruit power, reproduce components, or continue after loss of recognized operators were to be halted, isolated, or placed under multiple independent controls.

### C. Large-Scale Weave Extraction

Extraction beyond defined thresholds required shared measurement and could be suspended where depletion or cross-border effects appeared.

Later schedules added restrictions on mirror-memory systems, transmutation reserves, Fold laboratories, and the assignment of constructed beings to containment work without recognized refusal rights.

---

## III. Shared Knowledge Requirements

The Accords revived the Concord of Flame’s rule that survival knowledge could not remain private.

Signatories were required to deposit:

- shutdown procedures;
- known failure modes;
- operating dependencies;
- locations of linked systems;
- and records sufficient for another community to contain a project if its owners vanished.

These deposits saved lives.

They were also incomplete. States feared that a full shutdown record would reveal how to seize, reproduce, or sabotage an engine. Some supplied obsolete plans. Others divided essential information among archives that could not safely communicate.

The Accords recognized the problem of secrecy without ending the incentives that produced it.

---

## IV. Enforcement

No central sovereign enforced the Accords.

Inspection depended upon reciprocal access, neutral arbiters, technical witnesses, and the ability of other signatories to impose trade or passage restrictions. These mechanisms worked against smaller violators and failed where powerful states could threaten withdrawal.

Emergency exemptions became a central weakness.

A prohibited system could remain active if shutting it down posed immediate danger. Because decades of infrastructure had been built around such systems, states repeatedly argued that continued operation was the safer course.

In some cases they were correct.

In others, dependence became a permanent excuse for delay.

---

## V. Immediate Violation

The Accords were widely signed and almost immediately violated.

This fact is often used to call them meaningless. The conclusion is too simple.

Violations varied from concealed military projects to incomplete disclosures, late inspections, necessary emergency use, and disagreement over thresholds no available instrument could measure reliably.

The settlement did close some works, coordinate several evacuations, and establish the first cross-border maps of linked technical risk.

It failed to stop the Cinder Years by **2599 A.D.** because no document could repair decades of damage, dissolve political rivalry, or safely switch off every dangerous dependency at once.

---

## VI. Difference from the Concord of Flame

The two agreements are frequently confused.

The **Concord of Flame**, ratified in **2186 A.D.**, established labor rights, mutual aid, safe teaching, kinship recognition, and neutral arbitration among guild-states.

The **Ash Accords**, negotiated in **2594 A.D.**, imposed emergency restrictions upon dangerous technical systems.

The earlier covenant asked what makers owed one another.

The later agreement identified practices no community could safely claim as its private risk.

The Ash Accords depended upon the older Concord’s institutions even as they revealed how much those institutions had weakened.

---

## VII. Legacy

Later ages inherited several principles from the Accords:

- cross-border effects create cross-border duties;
- a system’s operators must disclose failure procedures;
- technical autonomy does not erase political responsibility;
- survival infrastructure requires a safe means of refusal and shutdown;
- and competitive secrecy can itself become a public hazard.

Modern institutions cite these principles when regulating delving engines, Fold research, or relic systems whose original makers cannot be consulted.

The analogy is imperfect. Labyrinth artifacts are not Ember machines, and excessive confidence in old categories creates new danger.

The Accords are most useful as a record of governance under uncertainty, not a universal technical manual.

---

## VIII. Ministry Conclusions

1. The Ash Accords were negotiated in 2594 A.D. during the Cinder Years.
2. They restricted high-order elemental work, autonomous spell engines, and large-scale Weave extraction.
3. They required shared shutdown and failure knowledge.
4. Enforcement relied upon reciprocity and lacked a central sovereign.
5. Emergency dependence made immediate compliance both difficult and politically exploitable.
6. Widespread violation did not erase the lives saved through closures, disclosures, and coordinated response.
7. The Accords did not end the Cinder Years but shaped later law concerning shared technical risk.
