# **The Ignition Catastrophes and Cinder Years**

### _The Cascading Failures That Burned Through the Promise of the Ember Age_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Catastrophe Reconstruction Bureau  
**Supporting Offices:** Environmental Harm Archive, Craft Lineage Commission, Fold Phenomena Desk  
**Primary Compiler:** Researcher Falyn Onarra, Cascading Rupture Studies  
**Contributing Scholars:** Professor Arav Dhal-Senn, Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Magistrate Talar Shellmark  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Multi-System Catastrophe Survey / Grade-II Uneven Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** emergency ledgers, ruined engines, aether residue, harvest records, weather reconstructions, displaced-person testimony, currency archives, and Fold surveys  
**Historical Range:** Approximately 2520–2599 A.D., during the late [[The Age of Embers|Age of Embers]]

---

## **I. Definition**

The **Ignition Catastrophes** were a series of magical, industrial, environmental, and civic failures beginning around **2520 A.D.**

The most concentrated period, **2571–2599 A.D.**, is called the **Cinder Years**.

No evidence supports one explosion, inventor, guild, or spell as the sole cause. The disasters emerged from connected systems operating beyond understood limits: aether extraction, elemental engines, weather control, transmutation finance, artificial labor, inherited Orichalcum mechanisms, and competitive secrecy among guild-states.

Some devices stopped working.

Others continued working after their instructions ceased to match the world around them.

---

## **II. Conditions Before Ignition**

The [[The Age of Embers|Age of Embers]] produced broad gains in heat, transport, sanitation, construction, harvest stability, and technical education.

It also created dependencies that crossed political borders while remaining governed by competing institutions. A city might rely on weather towers operated elsewhere, currency backed by transmutation reserves, and harvest engines repaired from instructions held by a rival guild.

Known pressures included:

- aether exhaustion near major works;
- alchemical pollution;
- deferred maintenance;
- incompatible modifications to shared systems;
- constructed beings denied authority to report their own failure states;
- deliberate suppression of defects;
- and extraction from ancient mechanisms whose original constraints were unknown.

The crisis was therefore neither a simple punishment for invention nor an unforeseeable accident.

It was accumulated vulnerability.

---

## **III. Redglass Burning**

At **Redglass**, windows and mirrors began melting before surrounding timber ignited.

Reflections continued burning after visible flames were extinguished. People attempting to cross mirrored streets were injured by heat corresponding to objects no longer present. Emergency crews shattered glass, only to create smaller reflective surfaces carrying the effect into drains and rubble.

The district’s workshops had combined light-storage, civic heating, and memory-glass technologies. Each system was considered stable in isolation.

Their interaction was not.

Redglass became an early symbol of the crisis because ordinary domestic material turned into a carrier of technical failure.

---

## **IV. The Cinder Years**

From **2571 A.D.**, disasters became frequent enough that recovery in one region was interrupted by failure in another.

Recorded effects include:

- aether storms crossing established containment lines;
- harvest engines producing sterile abundance or consuming seed reserves;
- spells creating mutually contradictory local conditions;
- artificial workers continuing abandoned tasks against evacuation orders;
- currency transmutation collapsing material value and public trust;
- and Folds appearing beyond laboratories built to contain them.

The term **Cinder Years** does not mean every place burned. It describes an age in which communities lived among the remains of systems they could no longer assume were safe.

Some regions suffered little direct damage and profited from others’ collapse. Several later accounts exaggerate universal ruin to conceal that distinction.

---

## **V. The Nine-Day Furnace**

In **2582 A.D.**, an industrial complex now called the **Nine-Day Furnace** entered uncontrolled operation.

Attempts to quench it caused the mechanism to draw heat from surrounding ground, buildings, bodies, and weather. For nine days the furnace alternated between impossible cold and destructive output.

Beastfolk Stone-Singers and Dwarven resonance crews altered the ground’s harmonic structure to isolate the complex. The intervention prevented wider spread but permanently changed regional Stone-Song.

Later songs describe the area as injured rather than dead.

The operation is one of the best-documented examples of cross-cultural disaster response during the Cinder Years. It is also a warning against describing every successful containment as restoration.

The furnace stopped.

The land did not return to what it had been.

---

## **VI. Constructed Life and Assigned Purpose**

Many Ember systems used constructed or altered beings for dangerous and repetitive work.

During the crises, some continued commands after their owners died. Others recognized danger but lacked legal authority to stop production. A smaller number refused assigned purpose and organized evacuations, repairs, or their own escape.

Contemporary guild records often classify these incidents as equipment behavior. Testimony preserved by workers and kinship houses treats them as decisions by persons placed inside machinery and denied the right to object.

This dispute influenced later law concerning created life, labor, and responsibility.

It also complicates blame. A system’s apparent autonomy may have been personhood, damaged instruction, emergent magic, or several of these at once.

---

## **VII. The Ash Accords**

In **2594 A.D.**, surviving guild-states negotiated the [[The Ash Accords|Ash Accords]]. They restricted high-order elemental work, autonomous spell engines, and large-scale extraction from the Weave.

The settlement slowed some forms of escalation and coordinated emergency knowledge.

It came after decades of damage, lacked consistent enforcement, and was violated almost immediately by states fearing that rivals would retain prohibited capacity.

The Accords belong to the catastrophe’s history because regulation was not a final act after the crisis.

It was one uncertain intervention inside it.

---

## **VIII. What Did Not Cause the Crisis**

The **Great Drift** occurred much later, after the Labyrinth’s transformation of geography. It did not cause the Ignition Catastrophes or the Cinder Years.

Nor does current evidence prove that the crisis repeated the [[The Sundering Silence|Sundering Silence]]. Both involve civilizational systems failing to reproduce themselves, but their material signatures differ.

Claims that one people, one guild, or one forbidden invention caused every disaster are products of later political blame.

Specific institutions caused specific harms.

The wider catastrophe arose because those harms could travel through shared systems faster than authority, trust, or knowledge could respond.

---

## **IX. Ministry Conclusions**

1. The Ignition Catastrophes were cascading failures, not one event.
2. The Cinder Years conventionally span 2571–2599 A.D.
3. Innovation improved ordinary life while producing cross-border dependencies and accumulated risk.
4. Competitive secrecy and fragmented authority made shared systems harder to govern safely.
5. Constructed beings were participants and victims, not merely equipment.
6. The Nine-Day Furnace was contained through Beastfolk and Dwarven cooperation at permanent environmental cost.
7. The Ash Accords reduced some danger but arrived late and were weakly enforced.
8. The Great Drift did not cause the crisis.
