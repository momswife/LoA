# The Wars of Making

### _The Ember-Era Conflicts Fought with Patents, Forges, Weather, and Stolen Skill_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Conflict Antiquities Review  
**Supporting Offices:** Craft Lineage Commission, Environmental Harm Archive, Concord Records Desk  
**Primary Compiler:** Professor Arav Dhal-Senn, History of Applied Making  
**Contributing Scholars:** Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Researcher Falyn Onarra, Magistrate Talar Shellmark  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Comparative Industrial Conflict Survey / Grade-II Guild-State Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** arbitration rolls, forge contracts, patent copies, apprentice testimony, sabotage inquiries, weather reconstructions, supply ledgers, and surviving neutral archives  
**Historical Range:** Principally the middle [[The Age of Embers|Age of Embers]], approximately 2260–2440 A.D.

---

## I. Purpose and Scope

The **Wars of Making** were conflicts among the guild-states and craft leagues that rose after the Empire of Law and Light.

They were rarely wars of mass territorial annexation. Their objects were ore, water, trade routes, skilled labor, inherited mechanisms, technical manuscripts, and the right to define who owned an invention.

The [[The Concord of Flame|Concord of Flame]] declared that survival knowledge should not remain private and that no person could be bound to a guild by inheritance. The creed saved lives and enabled cooperation.

It did not eliminate competition.

Publicly, guild-states praised shared making. Privately, many treated a single alloy, apprentice, or weather engine as the difference between prosperity and dependence.

---

## II. What Counted as War

Ember-Era conflict took forms later military histories often omit:

- recruiting or abducting apprentices;
- destroying the only complete copy of a process;
- manipulating ore and grain supply;
- flooding a competitor’s works;
- patent claims enforced by hired companies;
- sabotage designed to resemble technical failure;
- engineered weather over trade routes;
- and withholding safety knowledge during disaster.

Formal armies still fought, especially around rivers, mines, and ancient sites. Yet a forge could be defeated without its walls falling if its teachers left, its fuel contracts vanished, or every buyer believed its work unsafe.

The era made expertise strategic territory.

---

## III. The Brass Quarrel

The **Brass Quarrel** began as a dispute between Vastel and the Brass Republic over a self-cooling alloy.

Both claimed independent discovery. Surviving samples indicate related but not identical processes, suggesting the conflict grew less from theft than from fear that two producers would destroy each other’s monopoly.

The quarrel escalated through espionage, false defect reports, apprentice inducement, supply interference, and attacks upon foundries presented as industrial accidents.

No declaration of war survives. Neither side wanted neutral guilds to invoke mutual-disaster provisions under the Concord of Flame.

The conflict ended only after copies of both processes entered a neutral archive. The settlement protected access but did not publicly identify the saboteurs.

---

## IV. The Furnace Sieges

The **Furnace Sieges** followed when several forge communities refused to produce defensive alloys for rival city-states.

Besiegers argued that the Concord’s disaster obligations made refusal unlawful. The forges replied that compulsory production recreated imperial hereditary service under guild language.

At the longest siege, furnace keepers threatened to release molten metal into the river rather than surrender the works. The threat would have denied the forge to its attackers and poisoned communities downstream.

Mediators secured evacuation and supervised access before it was carried out.

The resulting judgment distinguished aid necessary for immediate civilian survival from production demanded for strategic advantage. Later guild courts repeatedly cited the distinction and repeatedly disagreed about its boundary.

---

## V. The War of Borrowed Thunder

The **War of Borrowed Thunder** centered on stormwright engines attributed to Oria.

These towers could divert dangerous weather and power urban works. Rival states sought operating keys, trained crews, and control of the high routes connecting the system.

Three towers were destroyed during the conflict. The resulting imbalance permanently altered seasonal weather across several districts. Rain failed where it had been expected, arrived violently elsewhere, and continued to follow fragments of wartime command long after the armies withdrew.

The episode is an early warning in the history of the [[The Ignition Catastrophes and Cinder Years|Ignition Catastrophes]].

The combatants treated the sky as infrastructure. The sky retained the damage.

---

## VI. The Quiet Patent Killings

Between approximately **2380 and 2410 A.D.**, inventors, copyists, and arbiters associated with disputed processes died in incidents officially classified as accident, duel, or private grievance.

No central conspiracy has been proven. The pattern is nevertheless too concentrated to dismiss.

The **Quiet Patent Killings** created a climate in which authors concealed discoveries, divided instructions among several households, or deposited sealed copies with neutral archives.

Those practices later preserved essential repair knowledge during the Cinder Years.

They also made technical verification harder. Incomplete instructions circulated without warnings, while guilds could deny responsibility for processes they privately controlled.

Secrecy protected teachers and endangered users at the same time.

---

## VII. Beastfolk and Brethren Participation

The Wars of Making did not divide neatly between peoples.

Brethren guild-states employed Beastfolk route-keepers, memory specialists, artificers, and negotiators. Beastfolk kinships contracted with competing cities while resisting demands for cultural surrender. Dwarven enclaves held technical leverage but faced renewed attempts to bind apprentices and extract inherited craft.

The [[The Treaty of Breath & Beast|Treaty of Breath & Beast]] made some cross-cultural contracts safer by recognizing oral and embodied law. It did not make power equal. Parties continued to dispute which courts, witnesses, and records controlled an agreement.

Several conflicts turned upon that question before they turned violent.

---

## VIII. Consequences

The Wars of Making produced important safeguards:

- neutral technical archives;
- duplicated safety records;
- clearer rights for apprentices to leave;
- arbitration between incompatible record traditions;
- and limits upon claims that an invention granted political sovereignty.

They also normalized industrial sabotage, competitive extraction, and the use of environmental systems as weapons.

When the first major ignition disasters began around **2520 A.D.**, guild-states possessed extraordinary technical power but little trusted authority able to restrain its combined use.

The wars did not alone cause the Cinder Years.

They helped create the incentives and secrecy under which catastrophe became difficult to prevent.

---

## IX. Ministry Conclusions

1. The Wars of Making were contests over skill and infrastructure as much as territory.
2. The Concord of Flame constrained conflict without ending guild-state rivalry.
3. Technical secrecy could protect communities while increasing shared danger.
4. The War of Borrowed Thunder demonstrates that wartime commands can outlast the institutions that issued them.
5. Cross-cultural contract disputes were material causes of conflict, not minor legal questions.
6. Neutral archives emerged from violence and later became essential during the Cinder Years.
7. The Wars of Making formed part of the political prehistory of the ignition crises but were not their sole cause.
