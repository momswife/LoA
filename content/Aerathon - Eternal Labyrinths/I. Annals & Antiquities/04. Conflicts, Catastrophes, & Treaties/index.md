## I. Purpose and Scope

This archive answers one question:

> **What happened between Aerathon’s ancient peoples, and what changed because of it?**

The surrounding divisions answer different questions.

- [[Chronological Timeline of Aerathon|Eras & Timelines]] supplies the continuous chronology.
- The [[Brethren Peoples & Traditions]] and [[Beastfolk Kinships]] archives record who ancient peoples were and how they lived.
- [[05. Figures, Institutions, Houses & Lineages|Figures, Institutions, Houses & Lineages]] records the actors and organizations that shaped events.
- [[06. Relics, Ruins, & Archaeological Records|Relics, Ruins, & Archaeological Records]] records what materially survives.

Section 04 owns the event itself: cause, participants, sequence, experience, settlement, consequence, and disputed interpretation.

Its records concern wars, campaigns, rebellions, purges, civil conflicts, industrial disasters, systemic collapses, treaties, concords, decrees, and political settlements. Most subjects originated before the first true Labyrinths. Later history appears only where necessary to distinguish an ancient event from a post-Labyrinth revival or consequence.

The [[The War of Two Heavens|War of Two Heavens]] and [[The Quiet Edict|Quiet Edict]] remain within Section 01 because they established the metaphysical conditions of later reality. They are foundational events as well as conflicts. This index links to them without duplicating their full records.

## II. An Event Is Not Its Era

An era dossier summarizes many developments across centuries.

An event dossier narrows the scale.

The [[The Age of Chains|Age of Chains]] explains how the Empire of Law and Light governed, expanded, and declined. [[The Echo-Wars]] explains how distributed resistance became coordinated rebellion, how the conflict unfolded, and how it contributed to the fall of Sol Oranna.

The [[The Age of Embers|Age of Embers]] explains a civilization of guild-states and technical experimentation. [[The Wars of Making]] records the conflicts produced by competition over knowledge, labor, and infrastructure. [[The Ignition Catastrophes and Cinder Years]] records the failures that escaped individual workshops and became systemic.

This division permits useful overlap without making the same article perform every historical task.

The timeline tells the reader **when**.

The event record asks **how, for whom, and at what cost**.

## III. Event Classes

### Wars, Rebellions, and Campaigns

These records examine organized violence and sustained coercive struggle. A single named “war” may contain conventional battle, sabotage, administrative seizure, famine, propaganda, forced migration, legal exclusion, and resistance carried through ordinary labor.

### Catastrophes and Civilizational Ruptures

These records examine events whose harm exceeded the institution, city, or system that produced them. Catastrophe may be sudden, like the Inversion at Sol Oranna, or cumulative, like the Cinder Years.

### Treaties, Concords, and Political Settlements

These records examine attempts to end conflict, rank authority, distribute obligation, or prevent recurrence. A signed agreement is not treated automatically as peace. Its legitimacy depends upon who could negotiate, who was bound, who was protected, and who retained the power to withdraw recognition.

One subject may belong to more than one class.

The [[The Fall of Sol Oranna and the First Withholding|Fall of Sol Oranna]] was a battle, uprising, infrastructure failure, religious crisis, and civilizational rupture. It is filed under catastrophes because the central event transformed every participant’s political world at once.

## IV. Evidentiary Cautions

Conflict records are unusually unreliable.

Victors preserve causes that justify victory. Defeated powers preserve innocence they may not have possessed. Treaties survive in official copies even when communities negotiated different terms orally. Casualty figures become moral arguments. Catastrophes destroy the instruments that would have measured them.

Five distortions recur throughout this archive:

1. **One-name compression.** Decades of separate uprisings are remembered as one war.
2. **Administrative euphemism.** Purge becomes audit, invasion becomes reconciliation, and forced relocation becomes pacification.
3. **Heroic concentration.** Collective action is credited to a ruler, general, martyr, or inventor whose name survived.
4. **Retrospective inevitability.** Later historians describe collapse as obvious to people who still had to make decisions without knowing the outcome.
5. **Treaty supremacy.** A preserved written copy is treated as more real than dissent, refusal, local amendment, or the lives of people never empowered to sign it.

The Ministry retains contradictory testimony where the evidence cannot justify one seamless account.

Uncertainty is not the same as neutrality.

Some uncertainty was produced deliberately by destroying the records of the harmed.

## VI. War and the Language of Civilization

Ancient powers rarely described their own violence as conquest alone.

Crown armies defended divine legitimacy. Imperial officials reconciled disobedient provinces. Guild-states protected invention. Beastfolk confederacies defended living obligations and ancestral territory. Rebels restored names, opened roads, or severed systems they believed incapable of reform.

Each claim could contain truth.

None erased consequence.

This archive therefore distinguishes:

- a participant’s stated purpose;
- the institutions that benefited;
- the methods used;
- the people compelled to carry those methods;
- and the outcome later memory assigned to them.

A just grievance does not make every act performed in its name just.

An unlawful regime does not make everyone living within it a willing agent.

## VII. Catastrophe and Responsibility

Ancient catastrophe was often described as punishment, fate, or failed miracle.

Material reconstruction usually reveals systems.

The Sundering Silence may represent several different endings grouped beneath one archaeological boundary. Sol Oranna fell because rebellion, maximum extraction, contradictory commands, divine uncertainty, and centralized infrastructure met within one overloaded system. The Cinder Years emerged because cities continued depending upon dangerous works while withholding the knowledge and cooperation required to limit them.

The archive avoids two simplifications:

- that every disaster had one guilty inventor;
- and that shared causation means no institution was responsible.

Responsibility may be distributed without disappearing.

---

## VIII. Treaty Does Not Mean Consent

Ancient agreements were shaped by unequal standing.

The Crown Concord reduced war by granting the strongest thrones authority to determine which smaller crowns were lawful. The Binding Concord preserved public order by allowing mortal institutions to declare old divine answers sufficient when no god responded. The Treaty of Breath & Beast protected communities while translating many of their claims into legal forms controlled by former imperial cities.

These agreements mattered.

Their flaws mattered at the same time.

Treaty records therefore ask:

- who possessed a seat;
- who selected the speaker;
- whether the speaker could bind every affected relationship;
- what enforcement existed against powerful signatories;
- whether refusal remained possible;
- and what happened when conditions changed.

The purpose is not to dismiss imperfect peace.

It is to understand what the peace required people to accept.

## X. Ministry Position

The Ministry recognizes the following principles for this archive:

1. Event records supplement rather than replace the era chronology.
2. No victory, divine claim, treaty, or surviving archive is accepted as neutral evidence of legitimacy.
3. Administrative violence belongs within conflict history even when no formal war was declared.
4. Catastrophe may be cumulative and institutional rather than sudden or natural.
5. A treaty may create meaningful protection without resolving the unequal power under which it was signed.
6. Peoples, households, and noncombatants are historical participants rather than scenery surrounding rulers and armies.
7. Later revivals must not be confused with the ancient events or organizations whose names they inherit.
8. The post-Labyrinth world belongs principally to the Living Atlas, except where era chronology requires continuous summary.
