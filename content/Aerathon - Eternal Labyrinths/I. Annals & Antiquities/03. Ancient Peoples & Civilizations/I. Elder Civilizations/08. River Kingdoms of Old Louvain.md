# **The River Kingdoms of Old Louvain**

### _The Settlements That Learned to Govern a Moving Country_

## **I. Purpose and Scope**

The name **River Kingdoms of Old Louvain** refers to the settlements, estates, islands, ferry communities, temple domains, and canal polities that occupied the central Louvain riverlands before the rise of the [[The Bell-Crown of Louvain|Bell-Crown]].

The name is convenient.

It is not exact.

Many of these societies were not kingdoms. Some recognized seasonal rulers, temporary flood magistrates, temple stewards, household councils, or no authority extending beyond a bridge and the farms that depended upon it.

They did not form one state.

They did not share one dynasty.

They shared a landscape that refused to remain politically still.

Later royal historians treated them as incomplete pieces awaiting unification by Queen Maelis Bell-Born. Surviving local records suggest the opposite. The river communities possessed sophisticated law, trade, water management, ritual, and mutual defense long before one crown claimed the whole valley.

Louvain was not created from disorder.

It was created by gathering many existing orders beneath one authority.

---

## **II. A Country Made by Water**

The ancient Seric and Marrow river systems did not follow their modern courses.

Seasonal floods created islands, erased roads, divided estates, and joined settlements that had spent the dry months as rivals. A ferry town could become the center of regional trade for one season and stand miles from navigable water in the next. Fields changed ownership when channels moved. Graveyards emerged from silt downstream of the communities that had buried them.

The riverlands therefore distrusted fixed borders.

Territory was described through use and obligation:

- who maintained a bank;
- who cleared a channel;
- who carried travelers during flood;
- who stored grain above the waterline;
- who could open or close a lock;
- who owed warning to settlements downstream;
- and who remembered where the river had run before.

Ancient contracts often expired when the water changed course.

Others followed the channel into its new bed.

The phrase commonly translated as **“law flows where water flows”** did not originally mean that whoever controlled the river possessed the law. It meant that lawful responsibility moved with the consequences of water.

A decision made upstream created obligation downstream.

---

## **III. The Peoples of the Riverlands**

The River Kingdoms were never exclusively human.

Human households formed a large part of many settlements, especially among ferry operators, farmers, soldiers, and local officials. Dwarven masons and metalworkers maintained locks, bridge footings, and submerged mechanisms. Elven surveyors preserved long records of shifting channels and forested floodplains.

Beastfolk communities were equally important and less consistently credited.

Aquatic and river-adapted peoples guided seasonal crossings, maintained spawning grounds, and carried messages through flood conditions that isolated permanent settlements. Shell-Ledger traditions recorded debts and boundaries in forms that survived immersion. Beastfolk wardens frequently negotiated between civic waterworks and wetlands upon which migration, food, and burial custom depended.

Later Bell-Crown records described these communities as auxiliaries to Brethren settlement.

Excavation does not support that hierarchy.

The riverlands were a mixed cultural region whose public works combined several systems of knowledge.

---

## **IV. Forms of River Rule**

No single political model governed Old Louvain.

Five forms recur in the surviving record.

### **Ferry Compacts**

Ferry communities governed crossings, rescue obligations, tolls, and the exchange of news. Their leaders were often elected for a season and could be removed for refusing passage during danger.

### **Island Courts**

Fortified islands supported judges, storehouses, shrines, and armed households. Their authority expanded during flood, when surrounding communities depended upon their elevated ground, and contracted during dry years.

### **Temple Estates**

Religious foundations managed fields, canals, burial grounds, and granaries. Later sources associate many with Oghma, Waukeen, Tyr, and related Dawn traditions, though the oldest divine names remain uncertain.

### **Floodplain Assemblies**

Agricultural communities met before the flood season to divide labor, repair banks, settle grazing rights, and determine where water would be deliberately released if defenses failed.

### **Lock and Canal Polities**

Settlements built around ancient or newly constructed water controls treated maintenance as the basis of authority. Their stewards were sometimes called **River Thrones**, though the title described an office or control station more often than a hereditary monarch.

These systems overlapped.

One settlement might owe religious duty to a temple estate, emergency shelter to an island court, tolls to a ferry compact, and labor to a canal assembly.

Old Louvain considered layered obligation ordinary.

The Bell-Crown later attempted to make it legible to one court.

## **V. The Bell-Watchers**

Beneath the riverlands lay the ancient resonant network later claimed by the Bell-Crown.

Communities maintained specialists trained to recognize vibration in foundations, water, suspended wire, drums, and metal bowls. They were called **Bell-Watchers**, **Deep Hearers**, **Under-Clerks**, and **Keepers of the Coming Tone**.

When a buried bell sounded, the Watchers recorded:

- pitch and duration;
- direction;
- water level;
- weather;
- nearby death, oath, or dispute;
- magical disturbance;
- and events that followed.

No settlement possessed enough evidence to interpret the network alone.

The exchange of Bell Ledgers became one of the first durable alliances across the riverlands.

Communities that disagreed over toll, worship, or territory still shared warnings because a tone heard beneath one island might predict a flood or magical failure downstream.

This created a political habit that later defined Louvain:

> A danger becomes governable when testimony from many places enters one record.

The Bell-Crown inherited this principle.

It also transformed shared record into centralized judgment.

---

## **VI. The Canal Collegium**

The **Canal Collegium** was not one academy and never governed the whole valley.

It was a changing assembly of engineers, priests, ferry representatives, masons, surveyors, and local judges who exchanged measures for locks, channels, bridges, and flood defenses.

Its earliest surviving bylaws require members to disclose changes likely to affect another settlement’s water.

The central rule states:

> _“No gate closes upon one bank alone.”_

Collegium measures contributed to:

- standardized depth marks;
- warning colors on bridge piers;
- common terms for upstream liability;
- shared inspection of locks;
- emergency grain storage;
- and the first river-wide schedule of navigable seasons.

Later Louvain jurists credited the Collegium with the first **Maritime Codex**.

That claim exaggerates continuity.

The surviving measures are practical agreements rather than one code. Bell-Crown scholars gathered, translated, and reorganized them centuries later, often removing local exceptions that had limited the authority of large estates.

---

## **VII. Blood, Locks, and the Red Canal**

The riverlands were not peaceful simply because their governments were local.

Wealthy households attempted to make seasonal authority permanent. Several bound lock mechanisms to household sigils or inherited magical resonance. The best-known surviving examples are the **Crown-Locks of Merrow**, though the name was assigned after the rise of the Bell-Crown.

These mechanisms allowed one family to control water beyond the season in which a community had granted stewardship.

Conflict followed.

The practice remembered as the **Red Canal** began during one such dispute. Later legend claims that a murdered river prince bled into a channel until every canal in Louvain ran crimson. Material analysis suggests repeated ritual dyeing, alchemical contamination, or both.

Whatever its origin, the Red Canal became a warning against private control of shared water.

Several assemblies required a lock steward to drink from the downstream channel before closing a gate.

The act made responsibility physical.

If the decision poisoned the water, the steward could not claim exemption from its consequence.

---

## **VIII. Queen Maelis and Unification**

By the opening of the First Era, the riverlands faced increasing conflict over bell interpretation, tolls, temple authority, and control of waterworks. Several settlements had begun converting temporary offices into hereditary titles.

In **44 A.D.**, the deepest known bell chamber sounded continuously.

The events that followed are examined fully in [[The Bell-Crown of Louvain]].

Queen Maelis Bell-Born emerged from the chamber with an authority no river settlement could ignore and no existing dynasty could claim as its own. She did not conquer every community at once. She offered a system through which their testimony, disputes, and obligations could be entered into one recognized record.

Many accepted because the alternative was continued conflict.

Some accepted only after military pressure.

Others retained local law while recognizing the Bell-Crown in matters of flood, road, war, and interregional judgment.

The coronation did not erase the River Kingdoms in one year.

It changed the direction of their institutions.

Bell-Watchers became royal officers.

Ferry records became petition records.

Canal measures became crown law.

Temporary River Thrones became offices held beneath a permanent throne.

---

## **IX. What the Bell-Crown Inherited**

The Bell-Crown described itself as the first lawful unity of Louvain.

It inherited more than it admitted.

From the River Kingdoms came:

- the exchange of warning records;
- the principle that upstream decisions create downstream obligations;
- seasonal and layered rights of land use;
- public maintenance as a basis of authority;
- ferry guest-right;
- canal and bridge standards;
- witnessed local assemblies;
- and suspicion toward any ruler who claimed ownership of shared water.

Royal government standardized these practices and extended them across a larger realm.

It also weakened many of their limits.

A local steward could be removed by neighbors who saw a bank fail.

A royal office could appeal to distant law, inherited privilege, or divine recognition.

The Bell-Crown made Louvain more durable.

It also made authority harder to escape.

## **X. Ministry Position**

The Ministry recognizes the following conclusions as the strongest current reconstruction:

1. The River Kingdoms were numerous societies rather than one dynasty or state.
2. Many were mixed Brethren and Beastfolk communities.
3. Seasonal water management shaped their law more strongly than fixed territorial borders.
4. The Bell-Watcher network encouraged cooperation before political unification.
5. The Canal Collegium was a distributed assembly, not a centralized Crown institution.
6. River Thrones originally described temporary or functional authority more often than hereditary monarchy.
7. Queen Maelis unified existing systems rather than creating lawful society from nothing.
8. Later Bell-Crown law preserved river practice while recasting shared authority as royal inheritance.

The River Kingdoms did not fail because they lacked one crown.

They became the foundation from which one crown learned how to hear an entire valley.
