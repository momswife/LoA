## **I. Purpose and Evidentiary Limit**

The **First Brethren** were not a tribe waiting outside the first city, nor a single generation who awoke speaking one language beneath one god.

The term describes the earliest mortal communities in which Brethren peoples can be identified after their creation.

Their origin belongs to [[The Creation of the Brethren]]. This record begins with the harder question: what did those mortals do once divine intention became mortal practice?

No eyewitness chronicle survives. Most written sources are later copies of marks whose meanings were already uncertain when they were copied. Creation hymns explain purpose more readily than daily life. Crown genealogies invented ancestors where the evidence preserved councils, work crews, or no ruler at all.

The strongest reconstruction therefore rests upon agreement among several kinds of evidence:

- names repeated across unrelated households;
- structures altered by people who did not build the original foundation;
- tools sized and shaped for several bodies;
- stores replenished through more than one seasonal system;
- obligations recorded in writing, carving, song pattern, touch sequence, and material placement;
- and repairs that changed divine designs rather than merely restoring them.

The First Brethren are visible most clearly where they answered the world instead of copying their makers.

---

## **II. No First Nation**

The earliest Brethren did not belong to one state.

They lived within foundation settlements, traveling work companies, temple precincts, mixed river communities, upland enclosures, and the first durable roads between them. Human, elven, and dwarven names occur most consistently. Other names cannot always be connected safely to a modern lineage.

The evidence shows movement from the beginning.

A person might be born beside one foundation, learn a craft at another, swear maintenance duty to a third, and be buried among a household unrelated by descent. Settlement membership often followed responsibility: a person belonged where their word, labor, care, or testimony remained answerable.

Later theologians called this proof that the Brethren were one people.

It proves that they knew how to cooperate without becoming one people.

The name _Brethren_ appears to have described this capacity. Its oldest forms are plural and relational: **keepers beyond one life**, **inheritors who may answer**, or **kin in the unfinished work**.

No surviving form means _children of one blood_.

---

## **III. The First Shared Settlements**

The First Brethren lived beside Beastfolk communities during the closing work of creation.

This conclusion is supported by the same evidence summarized in [[The First Making]]: fixed measurements bend around migratory paths; deep foundations preserve animal-marked handholds beside Brethren tool cuts; water channels combine permanent gates with seasonal overflow grounds; and public surfaces carry both Auric Line and signs intended to be sounded, touched, scented, or renewed through living performance.

These were not Brethren cities with visiting Beastfolk laborers.

They were shared settlements constructed from several ways of knowing place.

The best-known principle associated with them is the [[The Creation of the Brethren#VI. The First Law|Law of the Shared Wall]]:

> _“What shields more than one household cannot belong to one household alone.”_

The law did not forbid private rooms, tools, gardens, or household stores. It distinguished possession from consequence. A wall, gate, channel, warning bell, road, or storehouse became a shared matter when its failure endangered people beyond the maker's household.

The law was practical before it was philosophical.

Someone had to inspect the wall after its builder died.

## **IV. The Five Inheritances in Practice**

Later Brethren teaching organized early civic life around five gifts: **Name, Record, Craft, Oath, and Office**. The first communities did not express them as a single doctrine, but all five are visible in use.

### **Name**

Names established that a person could enter several relationships without being absorbed by any of them. The same individual may appear under household, work, witness, and devotional names. Name tablets were sometimes carried when a person left a settlement and amended when new obligations were accepted.

### **Record**

Records allowed warning and instruction to outlive a witness. The earliest ledgers concern stores, water, dangerous materials, repairs, births, deaths, and promises. They are less interested in triumph than later royal archives.

### **Craft**

Craft made knowledge teachable. A successful object was not merely admired; its method could be repeated, corrected, and adapted. Tool differences show that teaching crossed household and bodily lines early.

### **Oath**

Oaths allowed strangers to create defined obligations. The oldest surviving oath fragments describe what must be done, who may call the oath complete, and what happens if conditions change. Permanence was a possible outcome, not the definition of sincerity.

### **Office**

An office allowed necessary work to survive its holder. Early titles translate as **Gate Listener**, **Measure Bearer**, **Store Witness**, **Keeper Until Thaw**, and **Speaker for the Absent**. Many were seasonal or ended when a named danger passed.

The crown was not the first Brethren office.

It was the office that later claimed every other office beneath it.

## **V. Household and Daily Life**

The first household was not necessarily a married pair and their descendants.

Excavated dwellings range from small family spaces to long compounds built around kitchens, workshops, gardens, infirmaries, or shared courtyards. Burial and provisioning evidence suggests households formed through descent, adoption, apprenticeship, refuge, and common work.

Daily life was structured by maintenance.

Mortals inherited works whose original makers were absent, intermittent, or no longer willing to explain them. Children learned which marks meant danger before they learned ceremonial histories. Meals were organized around work cycles and seasonal availability. Stories named the people who repaired a thing as carefully as those credited with its first design.

The oldest play objects imitate tools but introduce deliberate impossibilities: wheels that cannot turn, bridges that fold, measures with two correct answers. Some scholars interpret them as teaching devices. Others believe First Brethren children were simply as inventive and contrary as their descendants.

Both conclusions may be true.

---

## **VI. Learning to Alter the Design**

The decisive achievement of the First Brethren was not preservation.

It was revision.

Several foundation structures show careful departures from divine or Orichalcum geometry. A channel was widened after repeated flood deaths. A ceremonial stair became a ramp. A perfectly resonant chamber was dampened so nearby households could sleep. In one settlement, an altar platform was dismantled and its stone reused to reinforce a public granary.

Later temple records called such acts damage.

Material evidence shows that the altered works survived longer and served more people.

The best-known story is the **Unfinished Tower**. A divine patron ordered builders to complete a tower whose weight would crush a Beastfolk burial chamber. The builders stopped and answered:

> _“You taught us measure so that we would know when a work was wrong.”_

No confirmed tower matches every detail of the tale. Its endurance matters because Brethren cultures remembered refusal as part of craft competence.

A steward who could not question a design was only another tool.

---

## **VII. The First Divisions**

The strengths of early Brethren practice created their first durable inequalities.

Those who kept records could decide which testimony entered the future. Those who held maintenance offices controlled access to necessary works. Skilled households could restrict teaching. Permanent structures could convert seasonal use into fixed boundaries.

Three changes recur in the late foundation record:

1. **Custodianship became title.** Temporary responsibility was treated as inherited authority.
2. **Records became permission.** Use that had never required written grant was declared unlawful when no grant could be produced.
3. **Instruction became debt.** Some teachers claimed a student's future labor as payment for knowledge already shared.

These changes did not occur everywhere or at once.

They created the conditions from which later cities, courts, craft monopolies, and dynasties emerged. They also sharpened conflict with Beastfolk peoples whose relationships to migration grounds, waters, burial places, and living territories did not always produce the permanent records Brethren offices demanded.

The first wall created shared duty.

Its gate created the possibility that someone would claim the right to decide who belonged behind it.

---

## **VIII. From First Communities to the Orichalcum World**

The First Brethren did not vanish when the [[The Orichalcum Civilizations|Orichalcum Civilizations]] arose.

Their households, work traditions, arguments, and mixed settlements became part of those civilizations. The Artisans of Accord expanded repair into public philosophy. Settlement councils formalized witness and office. Roads carried craft methods farther than any one household could control.

The transition cannot be assigned one date.

It occurred when inherited foundations became the centers of societies whose living customs mattered more than the purposes first assigned by their makers.

That was the First Brethren achievement.

They inherited unfinished works and made lives around them.

They also left work unfinished for others.

---

## **IX. Ministry Conclusions**

The Ministry recognizes the following conclusions as the strongest current reconstruction:

1. The First Brethren were several peoples and communities, not one ancestral nation.
2. Human, elven, and dwarven peoples are the most consistently identifiable, but the record does not exclude other origins or later membership.
3. Early Brethren and Beastfolk settlements were materially and culturally interdependent.
4. Name, record, craft, oath, and office were practiced in varied forms before they became a unified doctrine.
5. The earliest offices were commonly limited by task, season, or review.
6. Brethren civilization developed through alteration of inherited designs as much as preservation of them.
7. Stewardship, recordkeeping, and teaching created both durable public life and new means of exclusion.
8. No modern people, crown, temple, or institution possesses an exclusive claim to First Brethren inheritance.
