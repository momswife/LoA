# **Gnomish, Halfling & Other Brethren Traditions**

### _The Peoples Royal Histories Placed in Their Margins_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Historical Reconstruction Bureau  
**Supporting Offices:** Comparative Peoples Desk, Migration and Settlement Commission, Household Archives Project  
**Primary Compiler:** Historian Merrow Vey, Brethren Historical Review

**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Comparative Traditions Survey / Grade-III Fragmentary Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** Workshop fragments, failure ledgers, guest books, seed exchanges, path songs, deed cycles, migration strata, household rolls, hostile Crown classifications, and modern traditions used with chronological caution  
**Historical Range:** Late First Making through the closing centuries of the [[The Age of Crowns|Age of Crowns]]

## **I. Purpose and the Problem of “Other”**

This filing concerns peoples whose ancient histories survive unevenly outside the great Human, Elven, and Dwarven archives.

The word **other** is an archival limitation, not a cultural category.

Gnomes, Halflings, Goliaths, Orcs, Goblinoid peoples, and communities bearing celestial, elemental, infernal, fey, or mixed inheritance did not understand themselves as lesser branches of a complete Brethren design. Some may have stood among the earliest Brethren. Some entered Brethren societies through covenant, migration, adoption, intermarriage, political recognition, or reclassification. Some rejected the name while sharing households and institutions with people who used it.

Ancient records frequently renamed these peoples according to the work an official wanted from them. Modern names cannot be projected backward without caution.

The purpose of this record is therefore not to place unlike peoples into one residual category.

It is to restore histories that royal filing systems made residual.

## **II. Gnomish Traditions: The Question That Survived**

Gnomish origins before the [[The Convergence Courts|Convergence period]] remain uncertain.

Some Dawn traditions describe Gnomes as specialized [[The First Brethren|First Brethren]] made to endure unstable magic and discover variations hidden within orderly design. Other accounts describe small communities arriving from Material worlds where magical conditions changed abruptly and survival depended upon quick learning.

Both traditions may preserve later explanations for **mana resilience**, the broad Gnomish capacity to acclimate to or recover from some forms of magical instability. It was never immunity. Ancient workshops that treated Gnomish apprentices as expendable detectors left unusually high injury records.

The strongest surviving Gnomish contribution is a culture of **answerable experiment**.

Early **failure ledgers** record unsuccessful designs, false assumptions, injuries, unexpected uses, and projects stopped before completion. A failure described well enough to prevent unconscious repetition was considered useful public knowledge. A successful device whose hidden failures endangered later makers was treated as a dishonest work.

Several fragments preserve the ancestor of the modern **Third Question**:

1. What do we expect?
2. What would show that expectation is wrong?
3. Who bears the consequence we failed to imagine?

The question made experimentation a civic matter whenever risk extended beyond the bench.

Gnomish **open benches** periodically placed tools, unfinished work, and teaching beyond household rank. They expanded learning while creating lasting debate over livelihood, secrecy, safety, and whether powerful patrons would take freely shared knowledge without supporting its makers.

Curiosity was never the absence of restraint.

Its most respected form included the wisdom to stop.

## **III. Halfling Traditions: The Work of Return**

Halflings appear irregularly in the earliest Brethren record.

Some Dawn accounts call them keepers of the small continuities overlooked by grand designs. River, hill, and woodland traditions more often describe independent communities entering covenant with larger settlements while retaining local household law.

Royal archives neglected Halfling history because it rarely centered monumental building or distant rule.

Its records survive elsewhere:

- recipe books marking famine substitutions and cultural exchange;
- seed lines recording migration and marriage;
- flood marks cut at household height;
- guest books naming passage, refuge, and news;
- footpaths renewed by use rather than charter;
- and songs that preserve who left, who returned, and who kept a place viable between those events.

The best-known teaching is **the Returning Light**. A traveler carries a lamp into darkness. Great households ask what wonder the light revealed. The traveler's neighbor asks whether enough oil remains for the way home.

The lesson is logistical love, not timidity.

Halfling **guest-right** protected both host and guest. Shelter, food, information, and temporary peace were offered under stated boundaries. A guest could not turn hospitality into permanent access, and a host could not use generosity to create hidden debt.

The related **closed-door principle** held that a person could refuse entry, rest, or service without ending kinship. Its clearest surviving wording is late, but domestic architecture supports a much older distinction between common hearth and private room.

Home was not imagined as a place where nothing dangerous happened.

It was the labor of making return possible without making departure a betrayal.

## **IV. Goliath Traditions: Deed, Trial, and Witness**

Ancient Goliath traditions preserve no single written creation account.

Their sagas describe ancestors entering covenants with mountain, wind, thunder, and exposed places where shelter could never be assumed. Dawn scholars classified them as specialized Brethren. Goliath memory keepers frequently described a later alliance: the people were already themselves before they entered shared Brethren law.

The distinction mattered because divine-design language was later used to assign Goliaths permanent roles as carriers, soldiers, and frontier laborers.

Goliath **deed memory** preserved events through trained recitation, named witnesses, and public correction. A deed was not remembered only as victory. Failure, retreat, rescue, refusal, and return could carry equal weight.

A **Trial-Name** recorded a choice revealed under pressure. Legitimate trial was not manufactured danger. The most respected cycles condemn leaders who endangered the young merely to produce heroic identity.

Several traditions also preserve **story-right**: the person whose deed was told held the first right to frame its meaning. Witnesses could preserve what they observed, but not convert another person's suffering into public inspiration without answer.

These practices demonstrate why oral history cannot be reduced to casual recollection.

Its discipline was carried in performance rather than paper.

## **V. Orcish Peoples and the Refusal of Assigned Purpose**

Several Orcish peoples entered the historical record through separate migrations during and after the convergences that reshaped the old Material world.

Some arrived bound to war powers or transported by armies. Others came as refugees, farmers, caravan communities, hunters, craftspeople, and defectors from those same forces. Empires preserved military records more carefully than household life, then used the surviving imbalance as proof that Orcs existed only for war.

Orcish traditions answer through **unbinding**.

Communities publicly destroyed hereditary service contracts and reforged weapons into tools, hinges, instruments, memorial iron, and ordinary household objects. Ending an imposed purpose did not require erasing skills learned in order to survive it.

The **Open Hand Oath** required each party to state what they would do, what they would refuse, and how the obligation would end. An oath without an exit was treated as a surviving fragment of conquest.

Ancient labels do not map neatly onto the modern distinction among [[Orc]], [[Half-Orc]], and mixed Orcish identities. Cross-peoples families appear throughout the record, while official categories changed according to inheritance, military need, religious stigma, and the politics of the court keeping the register.

The archive does not allow an empire's manpower roll to stand as an ethnography.

## **VI. Goblinoid Peoples and the Three Doors**

Old accounts place Goblin, Hobgoblin, and Bugbear ancestors in Fey territories conquered by a power that reorganized different peoples into assigned military functions. Mortal empires copied those classifications because occupation became easier when body appeared to determine rank.

Goblinoid traditions preserve a different relationship through the **Three Doors Compact**.

Three doors of different height and shape opened into the same council chamber. The design declared that coordination did not require hierarchy by body and that difference in access could be answered without making one body the standard.

Goblinoid households formed in ruins, service passages, market edges, temporary camps, abandoned defenses, and cultivated land dismissed by distant rulers as empty. The ancestor of the modern **Useful Ruin Principle** gave standing to those already maintaining a place others called abandoned.

This did not make every occupied ruin a rightful possession.

It required a claimant to ask who had already made danger livable before invoking an old title.

Goblinoid military traditions remain historically complex. Some communities reclaimed discipline as mutual protection. Others heard every uniform as the return of the order that had assigned their ancestors a function.

Neither response is more authentic.

## **VII. Planar-Marked and Transformed Communities**

Celestial, elemental, infernal, and other planar inheritances existed before the modern registry terms **Aasimar**, **Elementborn**, and **Tiefling** became consistent.

People acquired such inheritances through descent, covenant, residence near altered boundaries, blessing, exposure, transformation, and the long consequences of powers imposed upon a household or place. Some founded distinct communities. Most lived within households that were not uniformly planar-marked.

Ancient states treated visible inheritance as evidence of loyalty to the power associated with it.

That assumption was never reliable.

A blessing did not create consent to divine office. An ancestral pact did not renew itself in every descendant. Elemental manifestation described how force moved through a body, not what the person intended. Infernal signs did not prove corruption; celestial signs did not prove virtue.

The strongest surviving household clauses share one principle: **an inherited condition may create needs and history, but it does not inherit permission on another person's behalf.**

Modern communities preserve this principle through the Right of the Unchosen Halo, elemental refusal traditions, and the Uninherited Clause. Those names are later. The argument is ancient.

## **VIII. Admission, Classification, and Brethren Membership**

Why were some of these peoples called Brethren while others with similar practices were not?

The answer changed by place and era.

Early membership often followed household, oath, shared work, education, or civic responsibility. A person could participate in Brethren institutions without claiming Dawn origin. In mixed settlements, the term might describe one body of law among several.

The [[The Convergence Courts|Convergence Courts]] made membership more formal. Attunement, license, and recognized testimony could matter more than ancestry. This opened routes into citizenship while excluding people whose magic, language, body, or memory practice did not fit the Court's measures.

Crown states hardened the category around sacred descent and useful classifications. Communities were admitted when their skills were needed and marked foreign when their claims became inconvenient.

The same people could be Brethren for taxation, alien for inheritance, and monster for war.

Historical membership is therefore evidence about a political relationship.

It is not a verdict upon personhood.

## **IX. Shared Contributions Without a Shared Essence**

The peoples gathered in this filing contributed substantially to ancient public life:

| Tradition | Public Contribution | Historical Distortion |
|---|---|---|
| **Gnomish** | Experimental candor, failure records, open instruction | Curiosity reduced to entertaining recklessness |
| **Halfling** | Guest-right, provisioning, local continuity, practices of return | Civic labor reduced to cheerful domesticity |
| **Goliath** | Precise oral witness, rescue fellowship, trial and deed law | Embodied knowledge reduced to strength |
| **Orcish** | Refusal law, mobile hearths, agricultural and memorial craft | Whole peoples reduced to armies |
| **Goblinoid** | Adaptive settlement, shared access, disciplined mutual aid | Survival in marginal places treated as criminality |
| **Planar-marked** | Boundary knowledge, covenant law, embodied magical practice | Bodies treated as proof of allegiance or destiny |

These are continuities, not assigned temperaments.

No descendant is obligated to perform an ancestral tradition for the archive.

---

## **X. Ministry Conclusions**

1. Gnomish, Halfling, Goliath, Orcish, Goblinoid, and planar-marked histories cannot be treated as residual Brethren culture.
2. Their origins include First Making claims, independent beginnings, migration, covenant, and later political incorporation.
3. Royal archives preserved monumental rule more reliably than domestic, oral, itinerant, experimental, or marginal life.
4. Gnomish failure ledgers made risk and error part of transmissible knowledge.
5. Halfling traditions treated hospitality, boundaries, provision, and return as public infrastructure.
6. Goliath deed memory was a disciplined form of historical witness.
7. Orcish and Goblinoid records must be reconstructed beyond the military classifications imposed by conquering powers.
8. Planar inheritance never established continuing consent, moral character, or political allegiance.
9. Brethren membership described changing cultural and legal relationships rather than one bloodline.
