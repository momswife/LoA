# **Brethren Relations with Beastfolk**

### _Shared Foundations, Divided Laws, and the Long Work of Repair_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Historical Reconstruction Bureau  
**Supporting Offices:** Comparative Peoples Desk, Treaty and Concordance Desk, Oral Histories Commission  
**Primary Compiler:** Researcher Falyn Onarra, Comparative Peoples and Beastfolk Social History  
**Contributing Scholars:** Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Historian Merrow Vey, Magistrate Talar Shellmark, Professor Arav Dhal-Senn  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Interpeoples Historical Survey / Grade-II Contested Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** Mixed-settlement excavation, oral cycles, Shell-Ledgers, Auric Line, road and water compacts, household records, Crown law, war testimony, treaty copies, and material traces of displacement  
**Historical Range:** [[The First Making|First Making]] through the institutional settlements preceding widespread Labyrinth emergence

---

## **I. Purpose and Corrective**

Brethren and Beastfolk did not begin as separate civilizations meeting across an empty border.

They lived in the same first stable world.

They built settlements together, formed households, exchanged knowledge, fought, adopted one another, made borders, crossed them, and repeatedly argued over which relationships law must preserve. Later Brethren archives reduced Beastfolk to neighbor, laborer, subject, enemy, or petitioner because those were the roles Crown systems knew how to record.

This survey rejects that framing.

Beastfolk were co-authors of ancient public life. Their own histories are preserved in [[Beastfolk Kinships]], but Brethren history must name the ways Brethren institutions depended upon, classified, harmed, learned from, and were changed by them.

This is not a record of what the Brethren did _to_ an otherwise distant people.

It is a record of a shared world made unequal.

---

## **II. Complementary Purposes, Not Fixed Natures**

Dominant creation traditions associate the Dawn Court's Brethren with deliberate works, teachable craft, written record, chosen oath, and enduring office. They associate the Old Court's Beastfolk with inherited relationship, embodied memory, ancestral place, ecological change, and obligations carried through living communities.

Later theologians transformed those emphases into opposites:

- Brethren as reason and Beastfolk as instinct;
- Brethren as civilization and Beastfolk as wilderness;
- Brethren as makers and Beastfolk as inhabitants;
- Brethren as lawful and Beastfolk as governed.

The oldest evidence does not support these binaries.

Beastfolk built cities, roads, vessels, governments, archives, and monumental works. Brethren maintained oral, ancestral, seasonal, and ecological obligations. The difference lay in which methods particular institutions privileged, not in exclusive capacities assigned by blood.

Creation traditions are most useful when they explain why cooperation mattered.

They become dangerous when they assign permanent social roles.

---

## **III. The First Shared Works**

The mixed settlements of the [[The First Brethren|First Brethren]] preserve both Brethren and Beastfolk design.

Foundation geometry follows stable measure, then bends around migration and burial. Water systems combine permanent control with seasonal release. Written marks stand beside warning forms carried through sound, touch, scent, body, and renewed performance.

The [[The Creation of the Brethren#VI. The First Law|Law of the Shared Wall]] emerged from this world:

> _“What shields more than one household cannot belong to one household alone.”_

Its principle was reciprocal consequence. Brethren measurement helped a wall endure. Beastfolk knowledge identified which flood, migration, root system, or living boundary the wall had to accommodate. Neither contribution alone made the work safe.

The law did not require cultural merger.

It allowed several peoples to remain distinct while holding standing in a work that affected them all.

---

## **IV. Different Archives of Relationship**

Many later conflicts were described as disputes between written law and no law.

They were disputes between forms of evidence.

Brethren institutions frequently preserved obligation through fixed records, titles, boundaries, office, and repeatable measure. Beastfolk traditions often preserved it through witnessed use, migration, genealogy, seasonal return, embodied performance, living markers, or relationships with a place that changed form over time.

Neither system was automatically just.

Writing could preserve a promise after powerful witnesses denied it. It could also freeze one moment and erase later consent. Living memory could adapt to changed conditions. It could also be monopolized by prestigious speakers or lost through violence.

The strongest mixed compacts required more than one archive.

A boundary stone might name a route whose current location was confirmed by song. A canal ledger might require testimony from spawning-ground wardens before a gate closed. A burial right could follow the dead even when a river moved the grave.

Conflict intensified whenever one archive declared itself the only form reality could take.

---

## **V. Shared Settlements and Households**

Mixed Brethren and Beastfolk communities were common in riverlands, ports, roads, border forests, mountain works, and trade settlements.

Their relationships included:

- marriage and partnership;
- adoption and fosterage;
- neighborhood kinship;
- shared worship;
- craft teaching;
- reciprocal defense;
- stewardship of common water, road, wall, or store;
- and refuge after disaster or war.

These households did not always call themselves mixed. The distinction mattered more to distant authorities than to people whose daily life already crossed it.

Crown law later required legal identity to follow a recognized Brethren sponsor, household head, or ancestry category. Beastfolk parents disappeared into servant lists. Children were assigned one people's law. Property maintained together passed only through the member a Brethren court recognized.

The resulting archive understates interpeoples kinship while preserving the bureaucracy that denied it.

Household evidence corrects that imbalance.

---

## **VI. The Recurring Points of Conflict**

Brethren–Beastfolk conflict varied by region, but five pressures recur.

### **Permanent Title and Returning Use**

A Brethren office recorded ownership or stewardship at one date. A Beastfolk community returned seasonally according to migration, water, grazing, spawning, or ritual. Absence was then interpreted as abandonment.

### **Public Work and Unequal Consequence**

A dam, road, mine, wall, or cleared field benefited one settlement while displacing another's food, burial, movement, or sacred relationship.

### **Record and Recognition**

Brethren courts demanded documents produced in forms, languages, or offices Beastfolk communities had never needed. Failure to present the required record was treated as failure to possess a right.

### **Stewardship and Ownership**

Long maintenance became a claim to exclusive control. Those who had contributed knowledge without holding office disappeared from the title.

### **Complement and Assigned Labor**

Differences once used for cooperation became compulsory roles. Beastfolk were expected to guide, scout, hunt, carry messages, or interpret living terrain without equal authority over the result.

These conflicts were political.

They were not the natural expression of incompatible peoples.

---

## **VII. Cooperation Beyond the First World**

Interpeoples cooperation endured after the first shared settlements fractured.

The [[River Kingdoms of Old Louvain]] depended upon aquatic and river-adapted Beastfolk guides, Shell-Ledger traditions, floodplain wardens, Human ferry systems, Dwarven lockwork, and Elven long survey. Later Bell-Crown histories ranked those contributions. The waterworks themselves do not.

The [[Iron-Mane Forgefiefs]] joined Leonine and Hyenid governance with Dwarven structural knowledge and volcanic craft. Dwarven participation did not make the Forgefiefs Dwarven, and Beastfolk founding did not make Brethren residents foreign to every local institution.

Road compacts, markets, religious sites, and the [[The Convergence Courts|Convergence Courts]] created further shared spaces. Some allowed knowledge to cross political boundaries. Others admitted Beastfolk only through attunement or license defined by Brethren authorities.

Cooperation should not be romanticized.

People can create excellent work inside unequal systems. The work proves relationship, not justice.

---

## **VIII. The War of Two Heavens**

The [[The War of Two Heavens|War of Two Heavens]] hardened mortal differences that the First Making had treated as complementary.

Dawn-aligned institutions increasingly presented permanent law, written record, centralized authority, and transformed land as the proper order of civilization. Old Court traditions defended ancestral relationship, living obligation, and places whose use could not be reduced to permanent title.

Mortals did not divide cleanly by people.

Brethren fought for Old Court communities. Beastfolk served Dawn temples. Mixed households refused both demands. Local leaders used divine war to pursue existing rivalries. Divine patrons claimed to speak for peoples who did not obey them.

The [[The Quiet Edict|Quiet Edict]] ended open divine war without dismantling the mortal offices, titles, censuses, and sacred claims built during it.

The heavens fell silent.

The paperwork remained.

---

## **IX. Crowns, Personhood, and the Closed Eye**

During the [[The Age of Crowns|Age of Crowns]], Brethren monarchies increasingly treated their own recognition as the source of lawful personhood.

Beastfolk communities with ancient cities, laws, faiths, and treaties were described as unplaced populations until a Crown assigned patronage, tribute, service, or restricted territory. Sacred ancestry gave Brethren rulers a language for ranking peoples according to divine approval.

The period is remembered in many Beastfolk traditions as the **Age of the Closed Eye**.

Not every Brethren community accepted this order. Dissident clerics, Elven witnesses, Dwarven lineages, Human assemblies, mixed households, and unrecognized schools preserved alternate claims. Some risked life and status. Others opposed one abuse while continuing to benefit from the larger system.

Resistance existed.

It does not erase institutional responsibility.

---

## **X. Conditional Recognition and the Treaty of Breath & Beast**

The late [[The Age of Chains|Age of Chains]] [[The Treaty of Breath & Beast]] is often called the first agreement to recognize Beastfolk personhood across several Crown legal systems.

The phrase requires correction.

Beastfolk personhood did not begin when Brethren courts acknowledged it. The treaty decided whether selected Crown jurisdictions would recognize rights they had already denied.

It allowed property, guild entry, and temple rites under recognized Brethren patronage. The **Mark of Breath** could permit travel and become a brand of conditional belonging. Reformers opened real protections while leaving the Crown's authority to define the protected person largely intact.

The Vel-Orann Betrayal revealed that weakness. Rights framed as permission could be withdrawn through an audit, accusation, or change in sacred policy.

The treaty remains important not because it completed equality, but because it records both reform and the structure that limited reform.

---

## **XI. Roads, Hospitality, and Later Repair**

The [[Pilgrims of the Three Lanterns]] created a different kind of interpeoples settlement.

Their lantern-hostels offered passage, provision, and peace to travelers who accepted the law within the light. Brethren and Beastfolk maintained the roads together, exchanged stories and skills, and developed guest tokens and waystone practices that later institutions adopted.

The Pilgrims did not solve land seizure, Crown law, or unequal citizenship.

They created spaces where strangers could experience a rule not dependent upon ancestry or patronage.

That achievement mattered.

Repair often begins at smaller scale than the harm. A hostel cannot abolish an empire, but it can refuse to reproduce the empire's categories at its door.

---

## **XII. Principles of Historical Repair**

No single apology, treaty, or shared monument can repair the whole history recorded here.

The strongest traditions of repair require:

1. **Name co-authorship.** Public works must credit the peoples and knowledge that made them possible.
2. **Return standing to living communities.** Possession of an old Brethren record does not overrule present descendants automatically.
3. **Recognize several forms of evidence.** Written, oral, material, ecological, and embodied testimony must be compared without forcing one to imitate another.
4. **Separate access from ownership.** Maintenance, study, or discovery does not create exclusive title.
5. **Review continuing consequence.** A lawful work remains answerable when water, migration, population, or knowledge changes.
6. **Do not make recognition conditional upon usefulness.** A person need not guide, labor, perform culture, or accept patronage to possess standing.
7. **Preserve refusal.** Cooperation is legitimate only where a people can decline without being declared hostile or absent.

These principles are not a modern invention imposed upon an innocent past.

Their ancestors are visible in the first shared wall.

---

## **XIII. Ministry Conclusions**

1. Brethren and Beastfolk were co-residents and co-authors of the first stable settlements.
2. Creation-era cultural emphases never established fixed intellectual, moral, or social capacities.
3. Many conflicts arose when one form of record or relationship claimed exclusive legal reality.
4. Mixed households and shared institutions were more common than Crown archives acknowledge.
5. Permanent title, public works, record requirements, and assigned labor repeatedly produced unequal consequence.
6. Interpeoples cooperation endured within both just and unjust systems.
7. The War of Two Heavens hardened political distinctions without dividing all mortals cleanly by people.
8. Crown recognition did not create Beastfolk personhood.
9. The Treaty of Breath & Beast produced meaningful but conditional protection and preserved the power to withdraw it.
10. Historical repair requires co-authorship, multiple forms of evidence, living community standing, and a protected right of refusal.
