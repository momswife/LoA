# **The First Beastfolk**

### _The Communities That Inherited the Living World_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Historical Reconstruction Bureau  
**Supporting Offices:** Comparative Peoples Desk, Oral Memory Commission, Orichalcum Survey Commission

**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Early Peoples Reconstruction / Grade-II Prehistorical Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** mixed-settlement strata, migration marks, animal-associated burials, renewed route signs, water covenants, oral sequences, household remains, and later ritual traditions  
**Historical Range:** Closing stages of the [[The First Making|First Making]] through the first generations of the [[The Orichalcum Civilizations|Orichalcum world]]

## **I. Purpose and Evidentiary Limit**

The **First Beastfolk** were not one tribe assembled beneath the Old Court.

The term describes the earliest mortal communities in which Beastfolk peoples and characteristic forms of living kinship can be identified. Their sacred origin is reconstructed in [[The Creation of the Beastfolk]]. This record asks what those peoples made of that inheritance.

No first-generation chronicle survives intact. The evidence is especially difficult because many early practices were designed to remain alive rather than remain unchanged. A route mark was renewed when the route changed. A council song altered when a new participant gained standing. A boundary sign might be removed deliberately after nesting, flood, or migration made the old restriction unnecessary.

Later archivists treated such change as proof that Beastfolk lacked history.

It is more accurately understood as a different historical method.

The strongest reconstruction rests where material, ecological, and oral evidence agree:

- settlements shaped around the needs of several bodies;
- migration corridors maintained through repeated passage;
- burials connecting humanlike persons, animal remains, place, and season without treating them as identical;
- authority marks that end after a task or season;
- adoption rites preserved across otherwise unrelated peoples;
- and obligations remembered through song, gesture, route, touch, scent, or recurring assembly.

The First Beastfolk become visible wherever a community organized itself around the fact that no life endured alone.

---

## **II. Creation Was Not a Single Awakening**

Old Court traditions describe Beastfolk creation through many animal kinships rather than one model repeated across different forms.

This does not require that every modern lineage appeared at one moment. The oldest stories themselves preserve divergence, convergence, adoption, and change. Two peoples with similar bodies might claim different sacred relationships. Peoples unlike one another might enter the same kinship because they answered the same landscape or inherited the same obligation.

The Ministry therefore distinguishes:

- **sacred kinship**, the relationship named in creation tradition;
- **lineage**, a broad continuity of ancestry and form;
- **people**, a community with shared history and self-recognition;
- and **political kinship**, a network created through covenant, adoption, route, or council.

The categories overlap without becoming interchangeable.

The Old Court's purpose, as later traditions remembered it, was not to make mortals who could imitate animals. It was to make peoples capable of recognizing civilization as part of the living world rather than its replacement.

---

## **III. The First Communities Were Mixed**

The earliest Beastfolk sites are not isolated from Brethren settlement.

They occupy the same river systems, foundation roads, upland refuges, coastal works, and Orichalcum precincts. The [[The First Making|Auric foundation record]] preserves structures made through multiple bodily assumptions: high and low entrances, dry and wet resting chambers, scent-cleared food stores, sunning ledges, night paths, broad turning spaces, climbing routes, and communal surfaces meant to be sounded or touched as well as seen.

These were not later accommodations added to a Brethren world.

They belong to the earliest construction phases.

The best-known joint intervention occurred at the **Fields of Namar**, where a fixed channel threatened a seasonal floodplain used by residents, migrating herds, and downstream communities. Beastfolk speakers refused the claim that the channel's builders alone could decide its operation. Brethren surveyors eventually altered the design, preserving both irrigation and flood passage.

Later accounts turned Namar into a debate between nature and civilization.

The evidence shows a dispute over which civilization could account for more of the lives affected by its works.

## **IV. Animal Kinship**

Animal kinship did not mean that a Beastfolk person was governed by animal instinct.

It named a sacred and cultural relationship through which people learned to notice particular forms of life. Herd traditions emphasized collective movement, distributed vigilance, and the vulnerability of those left behind. Burrowing traditions emphasized shelter, soil, hidden labor, and the danger of collapse. Predatory traditions argued over pursuit, restraint, territorial protection, and the ethics of power. Scavenger traditions treated aftermath, salvage, and the restoration of places others had abandoned as honorable responsibilities.

No animal kinship produced one personality.

Two Canine peoples could disagree about whether the pack protected freedom or demanded conformity. Two Feline societies could draw opposite conclusions from watchfulness and self-possession. One Ursine community might understand winter withdrawal as sacred privacy; another might treat it as a public cycle requiring communal preparation.

Kinship provided questions, symbols, and practices.

Mortals still had to answer them.

## **V. The Jackal Charge**

Among the most widespread early principles is the [[The Creation of the Beastfolk#V. The Jackal Charge|Jackal Charge]]: the dead leave more than memory.

They may leave:

- dependents who require care;
- injuries that require acknowledgment or repair;
- promises that remain useful;
- promises that have become harmful;
- names that must be restored;
- bodies and places requiring protection;
- knowledge whose meaning was never completed;
- and danger that the living must not pretend ended with its maker.

The Charge did not command blind obedience to ancestors. Its oldest forms ask the living to **hear, weigh, and answer** what remains.

That distinction mattered.

An ancestor's unfinished promise could bind a household to maintain a bridge. It could also bind descendants to a feud whose original conditions no longer existed. Beastfolk law developed in large part around deciding whether inheritance required fulfillment, amendment, restitution, transfer, or release.

The dead were given standing.

They were not given permanent rule.

## **VI. Territory Before Property**

The First Beastfolk did not live without territorial claims.

They defended dens, fields, water, migration routes, workshops, burial grounds, orchards, fisheries, hunting ranges, and places of refuge. What distinguished many early claims was the expectation that possession remained answerable to use and consequence.

A community claiming a spring might be required to keep it clean, preserve access in drought, and accept limits during spawning or migration. A route household could restrict passage during danger but lose authority if it permanently blocked the journey it existed to protect. A burial grove could be held against cultivation while still allowing gathering under agreed seasons.

This produced overlapping claims.

One place might belong differently to residents, travelers, animals, the dead, and neighboring communities. Councils existed to negotiate those relationships rather than erase all but one.

Later systems of absolute title found such arrangements inconvenient.

Inconvenience is not evidence of absence.

---

## **VII. Authority and the First Councils**

The earliest Beastfolk councils appear to have formed around specific needs.

Known offices translate approximately as:

- **Path-Clearer**, responsible for safe passage and answerable for unnecessary closure;
- **Water-Listener**, charged with flood, drought, and downstream testimony;
- **Hunt-Bearer**, granted temporary authority during collective pursuit or defense;
- **Speaker of the Absent**, tasked with representing migrants, the dead, or communities unable to attend;
- **Den-Witness**, responsible for refuge, children, guests, and household dispute;
- and **Burden-Returner**, empowered to end or redistribute an obligation that had become impossible.

Authority followed responsibility. Several offices could operate simultaneously, and none necessarily commanded the others outside its purpose.

This distributed model made rapid action difficult. It could also hide informal hierarchies: elders might control which ancestors were remembered, established households might deny that newcomers possessed standing, and a respected custodian could prolong an emergency to retain authority.

The most durable traditions therefore made the ending of office a public act.

Someone had to witness power being put down.

## **VIII. Learning, Childhood, and Adaptation**

First Beastfolk children learned relationship before abstraction.

They followed short routes before hearing the full migration history. They practiced identifying who depended upon a water source before learning the formal language of territorial law. They were introduced to the names of living relatives, adopted kin, significant dead, and dangerous promises as parts of one social world.

Play taught bodily difference without requiring sameness. Excavated mixed settlements preserve games using several heights, speeds, senses, and kinds of movement. Some objects reward cooperation among players who cannot perform the same task. Others allow a smaller or slower participant to change the field itself.

Adolescence often included a **first answer** rather than a universal coming-of-age ordeal. A young person accepted, refused, amended, or questioned an inherited responsibility before witnesses. The important act was not obedience. It was demonstrating that they understood who would bear the consequence of their choice.

This practice survives in altered form across several modern Beastfolk peoples.

---

## **IX. The First Fractures**

Living relationship did not prevent exploitation.

The language of kinship could be used to make labor appear voluntary. Elders could treat inherited duty as ownership of descendants. Territorial custodians could exclude newcomers by claiming only ancestral users possessed standing. Speakers for animals or the dead could invent testimony impossible to challenge. The refusal to centralize authority sometimes allowed powerful households to avoid collective judgment.

The **Unmarked Kin** appear in several creation-era traditions as people who rejected the sacred role assigned to their bodies or ancestry. Some left a kinship without joining another. Some formed communities around chosen relation. Others remained within their people while refusing inherited ritual office.

Their survival altered Beastfolk law.

It established that a relationship imposed without the possibility of answer was not kinship but command.

Not every later society honored that lesson.

Its presence in the earliest record prevents the archive from treating tradition as unanimous.

---

## **X. From First Communities to Ancient Kinships**

As settlement and travel networks expanded, local obligations developed into wider cultural systems.

Route councils became caravan federations. Seasonal gatherings became confederate courts. Household memory became archival practice. Weather observation became aerial governance. Shared workshops became forge settlements. Grief rites became circles capable of receiving strangers after catastrophe.

These developments produced the ancient kinships recorded throughout this archive.

They were not stages in one inevitable progression from wandering to statehood. The [[The Stone-Song Confederacy]] and [[Pride of Seven Roads]] solved different problems through different scales of belonging. The [[Shell-Ledger Houses]] made continuity visible in bodies and records; the [[Mourn-Howl Circles]] preserved a process that could end when mourners were ready to return to ordinary life.

Their common inheritance was not one form of government.

It was the conviction that power had to answer the relationships it affected.

---

## **XI. Ministry Conclusions**

The Ministry recognizes the following as the strongest current reconstruction:

1. The First Beastfolk were many peoples and communities, not one ancestral nation.
2. Ancient animal kinships were cultural and sacred relationships, not fixed personality types or a complete biological taxonomy.
3. First Beastfolk and First Brethren communities were materially interdependent.
4. Territory was claimed and defended, but many claims remained conditional upon care, access, season, and consequence.
5. Early councils distributed authority among several affected relationships and commonly limited office by task.
6. The Jackal Charge required the living to answer inherited obligations, not obey every ancestor without judgment.
7. Adoption, refuge, and chosen kinship were foundational rather than exceptional practices.
8. Beastfolk traditions contained their own exclusions, coercive inheritances, and internal reform movements.
