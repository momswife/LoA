## I. Historical Classification

The **Sky-Reeve Clans** were ancient Winged kinships that governed aerial routes, refuge chains, weather observation, and reciprocal obligations between the sky and ground.

Older records place them within the broad political category **Beastfolk**. Modern descendants and related peoples are properly filed as [[∅ Avianfolk|Avianfolk]], following the Avian Reclassification and the development of a distinct aerial cultural identity.

There is no contradiction in both statements.

The Sky-Reeves belonged to the Beastfolk kinship world of their era. They participated in Old Court councils, negotiated alongside terrestrial animal kinships, and organized authority through living relationship. Their descendants later established that **Avianfolk** better described their modern peoples and shared aerial inheritance.

Historical filing does not overrule living identity.

Modern identity does not require erasing ancient alliance.

---

## II. The Sky Was Not Empty

Terrestrial empires mapped the air as distance above owned ground.

Sky-Reeve law treated it as inhabited relationship.

Currents had seasons, hazards, feeding lives, migration, visibility, sound, and dependence upon mountains, forests, waters, and settlements below. A route included launch, landing, shelter, water, repair, warning, and the communities affected by whatever flight carried overhead.

The Clans maintained:

- high and low flight corridors;
- emergency landing grounds;
- cloud and cliff gardens;
- storm-warning relays;
- water and supply stations;
- agreements with terrestrial settlements;
- seasonal closures for migration, nesting, fire, or dangerous pressure;
- and rescue duties extending beyond clan membership.

Their authority came from keeping the route usable and answerable.

It did not amount to ownership of all sky.

## III. The Four Cardinal Winds

The Clans organized themselves through four great cultural currents.

| Wind | Core Obligation | Common Associated Work |
|---|---|---|
| **North Gale** | endurance, warning, and passage through cold or violent air | storm watch, high refuge, rescue, pressure reading |
| **South Zephyr** | renewal, warmth, cultivation, and the carrying of life | cloud gardens, seed routes, rain tending, seasonal return |
| **East Tempest** | change, invention, challenge, and dangerous knowledge | testing craft, new routes, lightning work, public dissent |
| **West Cyclone** | distance, mourning, return, and the costs of separation | far navigation, lost-flight record, funerary passage, diplomacy |

These were not biological divisions. A lineage or household could be adopted into a Wind, marry across Winds, or **Turn the Wing** after a witnessed change in responsibility.

The names did not describe four permanent weather systems. They organized the obligations required to live among changing currents.

At full assembly, each Wind was expected to challenge the others' blind spots.

A direction no one opposed was considered a dangerous direction.

## IV. Clans, Wind-Mothers, and Reeving

Sky-Reeve clans were generally matrilineal, though membership and office did not follow one simple rule.

**Wind-Mothers** held authority over clan memory, adoption, route rights, refuge, partnership recognition, inheritance, and ritual obligation. The title described a civic relationship, not necessarily parenthood, sex, or a claim that nurture belonged to one gender. Some were birth mothers; many were not.

**Reeves** maintained routes, inspected stations, carried warnings, adjudicated passage, and collected agreed contributions. A Reeve's authority applied to the current or station entrusted to them. It did not automatically extend into every household matter.

Other offices included:

- **Pressure Listeners**, who compared bodily, instrumental, and animal signs of changing weather;
- **Lower-Roost Keepers**, who maintained accessible refuge and relations with ground communities;
- **Farcallers**, responsible for signals across distance;
- **Water-Bearers**, who ensured aerial authority did not ignore terrestrial supply;
- and **Fall Witnesses**, who recorded missing, dead, and unrecovered travelers.

The system's prestige favored high flight. Lower infrastructure and ground labor were frequently undervalued despite making every celebrated ascent possible.

## V. The Pact and the Wind-Tithes

The Clans renewed their common law through the **Pact of the Winds**, traditionally assembled at the **Eyrie-Spire of Taellen**.

Each season, communities contributed **Wind-Tithes**. These were not payments to purchase good weather. They were demonstrations that those who used aerial systems had returned labor, material, knowledge, or risk to their maintenance.

Three well-attested grades survive:

- **Blue Tithes** — harvest songs, route reports, seed, cloth, food, and other contributions carried through ordinary trade winds;
- **Silver Tithes** — crafted conductors, instruments, signal pieces, and weather records placed or tested in storm conditions;
- **Crimson Tithes** — dangerous missions flown by consenting oath-pilots to repair, retrieve, measure, or divert a threat within active tempest.

Every tithe entered the **Ledger of Gales** with the giver, beneficiaries, risk, and result.

Later reeves imposed quotas and treated Crimson flight as proof of loyalty. The oldest Pact rejects compulsory sacrifice: a risk taken without meaningful consent creates debt; it does not settle one.

---

## VI. The Ledger of Gales

The Ledger was not one ordinary book.

Its surviving components include pressure-responsive leaves, feather-marked route sequences, metal and glass inserts, ground copies, and oral indexes carried by Farcallers. The central Taellen volume reportedly hummed or moved air when pressure changed.

Entries recorded:

- route opening and closure;
- tithe and repair;
- weather anomaly;
- missing flight;
- ground-community complaint;
- refuge use;
- adoption into a Wind;
- and unresolved disagreement among Clans.

Distributed copies prevented the Eyrie-Spire from becoming the only authority. They also differed. Modern scholars should resist combining every variant into one seamless original; disagreement was part of the record.

The Ledger's most influential rule reads:

> _No map of the sky is complete if it does not name where a falling body may land._

## VII. Craft, Aeroliths, and Ground Partnership

Sky-Reeve craft joined close observation with engineered aid.

Known technologies included pressure chimes, thermal markers, conductive kites, emergency descent cloth, adaptive harnesses, route beacons, lightning grounds, and **aeroliths**—worked stones whose resonance reduced or redirected apparent weight under specific conditions.

The earliest aerolith practice predates surviving Ember Collegia records. Later exchange with [[The Ember Collegia]] improved alloys, inscription, and containment. Neither tradition can honestly be called the sole inventor of every later skyship component.

Ground partners supplied food, water, metal, stonework, repair, weather observation, and safe landing. The Clans carried messages, seed, medicine, survey, and warning. Agreements governed falling debris, frightened animals, diverted rain, military overflight, and emergency landing.

Where those agreements failed, the sky became another direction from which power arrived without consent.

---

## VIII. Stormriders

**Stormriders** began as oath-pilots entrusted with Crimson Tithes, rescue, and testing dangerous routes.

They were admired because they entered conditions others could not safely face. Their oath emphasized memory rather than conquest:

> _“If I fall, let the sky remember my name in thunder—and let the road be safer for the next.”_

By the late Age of Embers, public races and prestige challenges had begun separating Stormriding from custodial purpose. Some riders pursued records while lower crews carried the cost. Others used spectacle to test new equipment, draw attention to neglected routes, or force conservative Wind-Mothers to acknowledge changing weather.

The famous **Skyraces of Daal-Ven** appear in later sources as entertainment. Earlier fragments describe assembly trials with prizes in route rights, repair materials, and naming responsibility rather than private wealth.

The distinction between courage and spectacle remained unresolved.

It would shape every later Stormrider revival.

---

## IX. Brands, Feathers, and Bodily Authority

Wind affiliation was marked through removable feathers, paint, jewelry, cloth, harness pattern, and—in some periods—tattoo or scarification.

The famous **Tithe Brand** was not universal. Early examples appear voluntary and commemorate a completed risk. Later authorities burned Wind marks into young or subordinate flyers, converting witnessed belonging into bodily control.

Clan reformers rejected imposed brands and restored the rule that a mark placed upon the body required the body's continuing consent. A person Turning the Wing could cover, alter, retain, or remove an earlier mark.

The rare **All-Wind** pattern indicated completed service among all four currents. Popular legend describes four living riders who bore it simultaneously. The surviving records do not support an exact count.

The mark demonstrated breadth of duty.

It did not grant command over every Wind.

---

## X. Taellen and Confederate Assembly

The Eyrie-Spire of Taellen served as assembly ground, archive, weather station, refuge, and technical workshop.

It did not rule every Sky-Reeve settlement. Its authority depended upon the Clans continuing to bring records, disputes, and tithe there. Ground stations and lower routes possessed their own councils.

Taellen's design remains uncertain. Sources describe a floating citadel, a mountain summit surrounded by suspended platforms, or several structures later remembered as one. Aerolith debris supports some sustained aerial architecture, but not every claim made by later Stormrider epics.

The assembly's most important act was the **Loosening of the Winds**. At its conclusion, each Reeve publicly surrendered emergency route powers. No office held at Taellen was supposed to become a permanent crown.

The rite explains the opening proverb.

The wind could be tended.

It could not be kept.

---

## XI. Decline and Later Revival

The ancient Clans weakened during the later Age of Embers as warfare, disrupted ground alliances, climate irregularity, and internal prestige competition made the full Pact difficult to renew. Several Winds continued as regional networks when Taellen ceased to host reliable assemblies.

After the first Labyrinths, anomalous weather shattered many remaining routes. Later accounts say the Eyrie-Spire broke apart during a storm of its own making. Surviving evidence cannot yet distinguish destruction of the ancient site from collapse of a later reconstruction.

Post-Collapse Stormrider guilds revived Wind names, harness traditions, and sky-law. Their work belongs to later history. They are descendants and interpreters of the Clans, not proof that the ancient confederacy continued unchanged for seven hundred years.

## XII. Legacy

The Sky-Reeve Clans established enduring principles of aerial civilization:

- the sky is a lived route rather than empty distance;
- flight authority depends upon landing, supply, rescue, and ground relationship;
- weather knowledge belongs to a network of observation;
- risk taken for public benefit creates a public debt of care;
- cultural affiliation may change without making earlier belonging false;
- and no emergency command should become a permanent crown.

Their failures were equally durable. High flight overshadowed low labor. Prestigious risk became spectacle. Tithes became quotas. Wind identity could harden into inherited expectation.

The modern Avianfolk inheritance is strongest where it remembers both the route and everyone who kept a place to land.
