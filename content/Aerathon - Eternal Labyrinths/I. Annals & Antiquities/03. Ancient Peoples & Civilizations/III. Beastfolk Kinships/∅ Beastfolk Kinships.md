## **I. Purpose and Scope**

**Beastfolk** is not the name of one species, nation, faith, or way of life.

It is a broad historical category applied to many peoples whose oldest surviving traditions placed unusual weight upon living kinship: the relationships among ancestors, descendants, chosen relatives, animals, waters, routes, seasons, and inhabited land.

The category is useful only when its limits remain visible.

Ancient speakers rarely identified themselves simply as Beastfolk. They named a household, people, clan, migration, watershed, council, or covenant. A traveler might belong to a Feline lineage, the Pride of the Dust Road, an adopted Vulpine household, and a rain-sharing compact at the same time. None of those identities was merely decorative. Each described a different set of people and duties.

Later Brethren kingdoms reduced this complexity to a legal opposite: **Brethren** were said to possess civilization, while **Beastfolk** possessed instinct. That claim is contradicted by every major body of evidence. Beastfolk peoples built cities, archives, roads, courts, workshops, trade networks, weather stations, and political confederacies. Their institutions differed because many were designed to preserve relationships that fixed title alone could not describe.

This archive records ancient Beastfolk kinship, household life, territorial practice, collective law, relations with Brethren peoples, and six especially well-attested cultural networks. It does not replace:

- the origin reconstruction preserved in [[The Creation of the Beastfolk]];
- modern ancestry records maintained within [[∅ Beastfolk|The Living Atlas]];
- the distinct modern filing of [[∅ Avianfolk|Avianfolk]], [[Chelonian|Chelonians]], and other peoples once included in broader Beastfolk categories;
- or the histories of particular rulers, institutions, wars, and relics maintained elsewhere in the Ministry archive.

## **II. What “Kinship” Means**

In modern speech, kinship often means descent or family resemblance.

Ancient Beastfolk traditions used the idea more broadly. Kinship described a relationship capable of making a claim upon conduct.

A person could be kin to:

- relatives by birth;
- people received through adoption, partnership, rescue, fostering, or oath;
- ancestors whose duties remained unfinished;
- a herd, flock, pride, pack, lodge, house, circle, or caravan;
- an animal population understood as an elder relation rather than a lesser copy;
- a route kept open through seasonal passage;
- a river whose flood sustained both settlement and migration;
- a place of burial, refuge, breeding, harvest, or remembered loss;
- and strangers temporarily protected by hospitality or common danger.

These relationships were not equal in every community, nor did all create permanent obligation. Their shared premise was that belonging became meaningful through response.

To call another being kin was not merely to express affection.

It was to admit that their condition could require something of you.

## **III. Ancient and Modern Classifications**

The oldest surviving Beastfolk records group peoples through **animal kinships** rather than the modern lineage taxonomy used by the Living Atlas.

Common ancient categories included:

| Ancient Kinship | Broad Association | Archival Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Herd Kinships** | collective movement, grazing territories, shared vigilance | Included peoples now filed under several distinct lineages |
| **Predatory Kinships** | pursuit, boundary, protection, challenge, and restraint | Never implied a single temperament or diet |
| **Burrowing Kinships** | refuge, soil knowledge, hidden passage, close household networks | Sometimes included peoples with no close biological relation |
| **River Kinships** | flood, wetland, crossing, seasonal return, and water memory | Overlapped with peoples now classified as Aquaticfolk or Scalefolk |
| **Scavenger Kinships** | salvage, aftermath, sanitation, survival, and social repair | The name was later turned into a Brethren insult |
| **Nocturnal Kinships** | night travel, listening, hidden labor, and alternate civic hours | Contained lineages now divided among Beastfolk and Avianfolk |
| **Great-Beast Kinships** | unusual size, long memory, territorial burden, and collective care | A ritual category, not a claim of superiority |
| **Winged Kinships** | aerial routes, weather, height, and refuge across distance | Most descendants are now properly filed as [[∅ Avianfolk|Avianfolk]] |

These categories were cultural and relational. They were not a perfect biological tree.

Modern classifications should not be projected backward unchanged. The ancient [[The Sky-Reeve Clans|Sky-Reeve Clans]] belong in this archive because they participated in the Beastfolk political and ritual world of their time. Their descendants’ later recognition as Avianfolk does not make that history false. Likewise, the [[Shell-Ledger Houses]] included Chelonian and river-associated peoples whose modern descendants may be filed as [[Chelonian|Chelonians]] among [[∅ Scalefolk|Scalefolk]] or [[Testudinan|Testudinans]] among [[∅ Aquaticfolk|Aquaticfolk]].

Historical categories describe the relationships people inhabited then.

They do not overrule how living peoples name themselves now.

## **IV. No First Beastfolk Nation**

There was no unified First Beastfolk kingdom.

The earliest evidence shows local communities linked by adoption, seasonal meeting, marriage, shared defense, migration, grief observance, and councils convened for a particular obligation. Some settlements were permanent. Others moved. Many combined both forms: fixed storehouses or ceremonial grounds supported people whose households traveled for part of the year.

Political scale did not always require a capital.

A route could join communities that never occupied one city. A song sequence could preserve law across several languages. A council could assemble at a floodplain, divide authority among those affected, and dissolve once its decision had been carried out. A household might hold responsibility for a spring without claiming ownership of every creature that used it.

Later crowns often mistook the absence of a palace for the absence of government.

The mistake was useful. Land without a recognized sovereign could be declared empty. Seasonal use could be described as wandering. Distributed testimony could be dismissed because no single person claimed authority to surrender everyone else’s rights.

The oldest Beastfolk political traditions were not less organized.

They organized different facts.

## **V. Shared Inheritances**

Beastfolk cultures differed profoundly, but five inherited questions recur across the ancient record.

### **Who Must Be Heard?**

Councils commonly distributed voice according to consequence. Residents, travelers, downstream communities, absent relatives, animal populations, burial keepers, and future users might require different representatives. This made deliberation slow. It also made it difficult for one ruler to dispose of a shared territory through a single signature.

### **What Travels Through the Family?**

Inheritance included names, craft, property, memory, routes, ritual roles, injuries, and unfinished promises. The [[The Creation of the Beastfolk#V. The Jackal Charge|Jackal Charge]] expressed the principle that the dead could leave obligations requiring care, investigation, fulfillment, amendment, or refusal.

### **When Does Care Become Control?**

An inherited duty could preserve a watershed for centuries. It could also trap a descendant inside a promise they never chose. Ancient Beastfolk societies argued constantly over whether an obligation remained alive when it harmed the people expected to carry it.

### **How Is Land Answered?**

Territory was rarely imagined as ownerless. It already contained use, memory, danger, dependence, and life. A claim became legitimate through continuing conduct: maintaining passage, protecting water, tending burial places, accepting seasonal limits, and answering for harm.

### **How May Authority End?**

Many early offices were conditional. A route-speaker, hunt-leader, flood-listener, oath-voice, or grief-keeper held authority because a task required it. Review, challenge, removal, rotation, and ceremonial surrender were therefore central parts of office.

No tradition answered these questions perfectly.

Their disagreements produced the kinships recorded here.

## **VI. Household, Adoption, and Chosen Relation**

Ancient Beastfolk households were often wider than a single line of descent.

Common forms included:

- multigenerational birth households;
- foster households joining children to seasonal or craft obligations;
- adoption following rescue, migration, orphaning, alliance, or personal choice;
- route households whose membership reassembled at recurring stops;
- grief households formed after disaster;
- work lodges and watch circles sharing food, shelter, and liability;
- mixed-lineage communities in which different bodies required different architecture and schedules;
- and households that included honored dead through names, places, objects, or recurring duties.

Adoption was not symbolic imitation of blood descent. In many cultures it created a distinct relationship with its own rites and limits. An adopted person might receive a route, witness, obligation, refuge, or right of challenge without surrendering earlier kinship.

Mixed households were ordinary enough that laws often specified which custom applied to inheritance, child fostering, burial, migration, and bodily autonomy. The existence of such laws does not prove harmony. It proves sustained proximity.

The strongest Beastfolk traditions treated chosen kinship as real because it was practiced.

The weakest used the language of family to conceal unpaid labor or demand obedience.

## **VII. Relations with Brethren**

Beastfolk and Brethren peoples shared the ancient world from the [[The First Making|First Making]] onward.

Their relations included cooperation, intermarriage, adoption, trade, religious exchange, shared government, conquest, enslavement, legal exclusion, rebellion, and repair. The two categories were never clean borders of ancestry. Beastfolk could live under Brethren civic law. Brethren could enter Beastfolk households and obligations. Entire settlements combined both.

Conflict hardened when Brethren institutions treated written title, fixed boundary, and a single authorized signer as the only evidence civilization could recognize. Beastfolk testimony often distributed authority among several relationships. A river compact might require assent from upstream keepers, downstream households, seasonal travelers, and the people charged with the dead. A crown demanding one negotiator interpreted this as disorder—or selected one speaker and pretended the others had consented.

The [[The War of Two Heavens|War of Two Heavens]] turned these political differences into divine allegiance. The [[The Quiet Edict|Quiet Edict]] ended open divine intervention without repairing the mortal hierarchies left behind. The later [[The Treaty of Breath & Beast]] recognized certain protections while forcing many Beastfolk claims into Brethren legal forms.

These relations are examined from both archival directions in [[Brethren Relations with Beastfolk]] and [[Beastfolk Relations with Brethren]].

Six ancient networks preserve especially rich evidence of Beastfolk law and cultural exchange.

None represented all Beastfolk.

Each became influential because its practices could cross the borders of one lineage.

## **IX. Archival Cautions**

Beastfolk history reaches the Ministry through unequal preservation.

Stone, metal, and royal paper survive more readily than scent marks, renewed paths, seasonal shelters, spoken councils, temporary boundary signs, and performances meant to change with their witnesses. Later clerks often copied the final decision while omitting the relationships that made it legitimate.

Three distortions require particular caution:

1. **Instinct language.** Brethren observers frequently described learned Beastfolk customs as instinct when they did not understand the teaching that sustained them.
2. **Empty-land claims.** Seasonal absence was treated as abandonment even where communities returned through well-attested cycles.
3. **Revival confusion.** Several ancient names were adopted again after the first Labyrinths. A post-Collapse revival may preserve genuine tradition without being identical to the ancient institution whose name it carries.

The purpose of reconstruction is not to make the record seamless.

It is to show where later certainty exceeded the evidence.
