# The Ravelmane Claimants and Seven-Ring Lineages

### _The Families, Oath-Kin, Custodians, and Pretenders Who Inherited a Forbidden Kingdom_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Dynastic Records Desk  
**Supporting Offices:** Comparative Peoples Bureau, Western Suppression Archive, Oral Memory Bureau  
**Primary Compiler:** Researcher Falyn Onarra, Beastfolk Memory Systems and Suppressed Histories  
**Contributing Scholars:** Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Magistrate Talar Shellmark, Historian Merrow Vey  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Contested Claimant Traditions / Grade-III Fragmentary and Suppressed Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** oral pride lineages, ring emblems, refugee household records, imperial confiscation rolls, the Tharos Interdict, oath fragments, hostile Crown annals, and later claimant genealogies  
**Historical Range:** From the fall of the Seven-Ring Kingdom in 927 A.D. through the pre-Labyrinth successor traditions that claimed its memory

---

## I. Subject and Terminology

The **Ravelmane Claimants and Seven-Ring Lineages** are the people and households who asserted an inherited relationship to [[Tharos Ravelmane, the Seven-Ringed King]], his western kingdom, or the [[Tharos Ravelmane, the Seven-Ringed King#IV. The Oath of Many Voices|Oath of Many Voices]].

They were never one dynasty.

Some claimed bodily descent from Tharos. Some descended from officers, council speakers, gate keepers, refugee hosts, or communities joined to the seven rings. Some inherited objects or oral duties. Some adopted the emblem as a political promise. Others manufactured a connection for rank, rebellion, or profit.

The Ministry uses **claimant** as a descriptive term.

It does not imply that the claim was false, hereditary, sovereign, or accepted by every community the old kingdom contained.

---

## II. The Missing Royal Genealogy

No reliable genealogy of Tharos survives.

Later songs name spouses, siblings, children, cubs, oath-daughters, war-sons, and successors, but the traditions conflict on number, kinship, species, and fate. This is partly the result of suppression. It also reflects Beastfolk and mixed-community kinship that imperial record keepers reduced poorly.

Terms translated as son or daughter may indicate:

- bodily descent;
- adoption into a pride or household;
- apprenticeship;
- military sponsorship;
- rescue and mutual obligation;
- or a person authorized to carry another’s voice.

Imperial investigators demanded a single bloodline because such a line could be identified, disinherited, and extinguished.

The Seven-Ring traditions often preserved succession as distributed relationship instead.

---

## III. The Seven Rings

[[The Seven Rings of Tharos|The kingdom’s seven rings]] may have been physical regalia, territorial seals, council emblems, covenant bands, or several of these at once.

Surviving traditions associate them with functions rather than ranks:

- passage;
- provision;
- warning;
- memory;
- judgment;
- refuge;
- and shared defense.

No complete authenticated set has been recovered.

Objects claimed as individual rings differ in material, scale, inscription, and date. Some are plainly later memorial pieces. Others contain alloys or craft consistent with the ninth-century west but lack secure provenance.

The absence of a complete set did not end the tradition.

Households carried ring tattoos, knotted cords, seven-part names, door carvings, circular scars, and spoken lists. What began as regalia—or was later remembered as regalia—became a portable structure for obligation.

## IV. Five Common Kinds of Claim

The archive distinguishes five recurring forms.

### 1. Familial Claim

Descent from Tharos or a named member of his household.

Such claims may preserve genuine kinship, but no unbroken line presently meets a documentary standard sufficient to establish exclusive descent.

### 2. Oath Claim

Inheritance through a community that swore the Oath of Many Voices or accepted a duty under it.

This form is often better supported culturally than genealogically.

### 3. Custodial Claim

Authority based on preserving a ring, record, gate, route, song, burial place, or refugee name.

Custody can be genuine without conferring rule.

### 4. Political Claim

Use of the seven rings as a commitment to refuge, plural law, western autonomy, or resistance to sacred monarchy.

Political inheritance may honor the old kingdom without continuing its institution.

### 5. Counterfeit Claim

Fabricated genealogy or regalia used to demand tribute, leadership, marriage, relic access, or public loyalty.

Counterfeit objects can nevertheless acquire later communal meaning. Fraudulent origin and genuine subsequent tradition are not mutually exclusive.

## V. The Night of Open Gates

The Seven-Ring Kingdom fell in **927 A.D.** during the [[Tharos Ravelmane, the Seven-Ringed King#VII. The Night of Open Gates|Night of Open Gates]].

The surviving account indicates internal betrayal joined to intervention by Crown-aligned forces. Gates were opened, defenses failed in sequence, records burned, and protected populations scattered.

Tharos’s fate is unknown.

The collapse fractured inheritance immediately. Different groups escaped with different things:

- household names;
- pieces of the oath;
- council procedures;
- children identified as heirs;
- military signs;
- ring objects;
- and stories of who had betrayed whom.

No refugee group possessed the entire kingdom.

Each preserved the portion required for survival and sometimes presented that portion as the whole.

---

## VI. The Tharos Interdict

The **Tharos Interdict of 1137 A.D.** prohibited public veneration, display of the seven-ring sign, teaching of approved royal narratives outside censored forms, and certain gatherings of western Beastfolk lineages.

The interdict followed the imperial settlement and accompanied the broader Western Pacifications.

Its purpose was not merely to erase one king.

The memory of a multi-people refuge challenged the claim that political unity required the sacred blood of a High Throne or the universal classification of empire. Even a flawed Seven-Ring Kingdom demonstrated that different legal traditions could coordinate without being made identical.

Suppression altered the evidence now available.

Gaps in claimant genealogies are not proof of fraud when the state punished preservation. They are also not proof that every later claim is true.

The archive must avoid using imperial destruction first to remove evidence and then to declare the survivors unauthenticated.

---

## VII. Lineages of Voice rather than Blood

Several traditions transmit descent through the right and duty to speak a missing community’s part in council.

A household might preserve the **Voice of the Open Gate**, the **Voice of Winter Stores**, or the **Voice of the Hunted Road**. The holder was not necessarily related to the prior speaker. Selection could follow apprenticeship, rescue, adoption, communal confidence, or the transfer of a memory object.

These are lineages in the archival sense.

They preserve a relationship across generations through recognized transmission.

Imperial clerks often recorded Voice holders as false nobles because they claimed connection without blood. Seven-Ring communities often regarded a person who carried the duty but lacked ancestry as more legitimate than a bodily descendant who refused the oath.

This conflict explains many apparently contradictory claimant rolls.

---

## VIII. Claimants and Refugee Power

Not every claimant served the displaced.

Some households used a Ravelmane name to demand obedience from refugees, arrange advantageous marriages, or centralize control of western routes. Some promised restoration while preserving the same species hierarchy and elite privilege the remembered kingdom was said to oppose.

Others maintained guest-right, carried warning between communities, preserved forbidden Beastfolk names, and defended people whom Crown and imperial systems classified as Unplaced.

The question is not whether a claimant possessed the correct symbol.

It is what relationship the claimant maintained with those on whose memory the claim depended.

Seven rings displayed above a closed gate invert the inheritance they invoke.

---

## IX. The Problem of Exclusive Succession

Later claimants often argued that only one line could be lawful.

The historical kingdom offers little support for that assumption.

The Oath of Many Voices appears to have coordinated several peoples, courts, and kinds of authority. Tharos may have been sovereign, war leader, chief arbiter, symbolic center, or all four. Even if one biological descendant could be established, that person would inherit relationship to one participant in a plural settlement—not ownership of every voice within it.

An exclusive Ravelmane dynasty may be a later attempt to make the Seven-Ring Kingdom resemble the High Thrones that opposed it.

The most credible traditions preserve divided custody and reciprocal obligation.

That credibility does not exempt them from scrutiny. Distributed authority can conceal informal elites as readily as a throne can.

---

## X. Material and Oral Verification

Evaluation requires several kinds of evidence.

The Ministry compares:

- material dating of alleged regalia;
- route and settlement knowledge embedded in oral cycles;
- household relationships across independent communities;
- linguistic forms predating imperial censorship;
- confiscation lists and hostile records;
- burial practice;
- and whether a tradition preserves unflattering details unlikely to serve a simple legitimacy claim.

No one category is decisive.

An old ring can be stolen. A new song can carry an old duty. A hostile imperial list can preserve a name accurately while lying about its meaning.

Verification should establish the kind of continuity being claimed rather than force every inheritance into genealogy.

---

## XI. Ministry Conclusions

1. No complete and reliable biological genealogy of Tharos Ravelmane survives.
2. Seven-Ring inheritance included family, oath, custody, office, political memory, and fabrication.
3. Beastfolk and mixed-community kinship terms were repeatedly narrowed or mistranslated by imperial investigators.
4. The seven rings functioned as a portable structure of obligation even where physical regalia were lost.
5. The Night of Open Gates scattered different portions of the kingdom among different survivors.
6. The Tharos Interdict deliberately damaged the evidence by which later claims could be tested.
7. A gap caused by suppression neither disproves nor proves a claimant tradition.
8. Lineages of voice can represent genuine succession without biological descent.
9. Possession of a name, ring, or ancestry does not confer authority over every people once joined to the kingdom.
10. No modern Ravelmane claim establishes automatic sovereignty.
