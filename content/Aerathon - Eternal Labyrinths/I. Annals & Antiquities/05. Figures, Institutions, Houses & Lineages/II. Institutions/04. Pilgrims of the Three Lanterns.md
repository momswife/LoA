# The Pilgrims of the Three Lanterns

### _The Interpeoples Fellowship That Made Hospitality into Road Infrastructure_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Historical Institutions Bureau  
**Supporting Offices:** Roadlore Archive, Comparative Peoples Bureau, Guest-Right Commission  
**Primary Compiler:** Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Institutional Memory and Pre-Labyrinth Authority  
**Contributing Scholars:** Researcher Falyn Onarra, Magistrate Talar Shellmark, Curator Rook-of-Rings  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Document Class:** _Interpeoples Road Institution / Grade-II Distributed Cultural Record_  
**Primary Evidence:** hostel rules, guest tokens, waystone sequences, oral road cycles, municipal light-tax rolls, traveler testimony, and later courier charters  
**Historical Range:** Late [[The Age of Chains|Age of Chains]] through the early [[The Age of Embers|Age of Embers]], with later revivals and claimed successors

## I. Institutional Character

The **Pilgrims of the Three Lanterns** were not a kingdom, church, ancestry, or single road guild.

They were a fellowship of travelers, hostel keepers, mendicants, craftspeople, former clergy, and route custodians who treated safe passage as a public obligation.

Their lantern-hostels offered food, warmth, message exchange, and temporary refuge to anyone accepting the peace of the light.

The three lanterns represented:

1. **Passage** — no traveler should be trapped because one jurisdiction refused the next road.
2. **Provision** — safety required food, water, warmth, repair, and truthful warning.
3. **Peace** — violence brought from outside had to stop within the recognized light.

The Pilgrims built a durable institution without making one sovereign law govern every traveler.

---

## II. Origins in Flight

The fellowship emerged among traders, fugitives, road clergy, discharged soldiers, displaced Beastfolk, and others moving during the later temple purges and provincial conflicts of the Age of Chains.

Its founder tradition centers upon **Yarrow the Kind**.

Yarrow is said to have lit three lamps beside a Louvain–Vinyot road for three people who died in one day without shelter. When he returned a year later, travelers had added lights of their own.

No contemporary record proves one founder or one road.

Several regions preserve different Yarrow figures, genders, ancestries, and causes of death. **Yarrow** may have been a personal name, an office carried by hostel founders, or a plant-and-road symbol later turned into biography.

The repeated story preserves the institution’s central truth:

One act became public law only because strangers continued it when the first keeper left.

---

## III. The Law within the Light

A recognized hostel established a temporary jurisdiction called **the Lantern Peace**.

Common rules required a guest to:

- secure or declare weapons;
- give a usable name, route-name, or accountable witness;
- accept separation from an active enemy;
- contribute service, story, warning, token, or provision when able;
- and leave the shelter no less safe for the next traveler.

Hosts owed:

- water and minimum food;
- truthful road warning;
- protection from violence within the light;
- privacy from ordinary pursuit;
- and equal application of the hostel’s limits.

Guest-right did not erase outside law. A murderer could still face judgment after departure. The hostel delayed violence long enough for rest, mediation, escort, or safe surrender.

---

## IV. Lantern-Hostels

Hostels—often called **Hearthlights**—served as inn, sanctuary, repair point, message exchange, and witness station.

They varied widely.

Some were stone compounds near cities. Others were tents, cave shelters, ferry houses, elevated platforms, or a marked circle around a maintained fire. Recognition depended less upon architecture than upon a keeper, three visible lights, posted obligations, and connection to another hostel willing to honor its tokens.

Mixed staffs were common. Brethren record practice joined Beastfolk route memory, scent warning, seasonal knowledge, and kinship hospitality.

The institution did not belong wholly to either people.

Later Brethren histories often described Beastfolk as guests. Many of the roads and shelters had been maintained by Beastfolk communities before the Pilgrim name existed.

---

## V. Guest Tokens and Waystones

Guests commonly left a carved token, marked coin, knot, shell, stamped scrap, or witnessed name.

The token could show that a traveler had contributed, carried warning, owed return service, or remained missing between two lights. It was not originally a passport establishing personhood.

Some municipal networks later copied tokens into centralized rolls and denied aid to travelers without accepted proof. The Pilgrim principle that no one should die outside because they lacked a record weakened wherever the token became the condition of shelter.

Waystones marked distance, hazard, water, jurisdiction, and the status of the next light. Reflective salts, chimes, scent markers, texture, and knot-script made routes usable under different bodies and conditions.

Accessibility was practical road law, not ceremonial generosity.

---

## VI. Social and Political Role

The Lantern network created spaces where Brethren and Beastfolk exchanged stories, recipes, law, craft, worship, and warning without first resolving every political dispute.

It supported:

- mixed caravans;
- escaped families;
- itinerant teachers;
- healers and repair crews;
- treaty messengers;
- mourners carrying the dead;
- and people whose identity was unsafe in a provincial registry.

The Pilgrims did not end land seizure, unequal citizenship, Crown law, or interpeoples violence.

A safe hostel could stand upon a road built through displaced territory. City-funded keepers could favor merchants over fugitives. Guest peace sometimes returned vulnerable people to danger once the final light ended.

Hospitality limited violence.

It did not settle justice beyond the threshold.

---

## VII. Recognition and Light Tax

Cities eventually recognized the network as necessary infrastructure.

Public support paid for grain, oil, repairs, bridges, and winter shelter. Contributions became known as **light tax**.

Funding expanded the system while changing it.

Municipal authorities demanded approved keepers, standardized tokens, traveler lists, and priority for official couriers. Several hostels refused support rather than surrender sanctuary records. Others accepted oversight because unpaid hospitality had exhausted the communities providing it.

The dispute had no pure side.

An institution cannot survive indefinitely by treating its keepers’ labor as free. Public funding can also convert refuge into an inspection station.

---

## VIII. Decline and Absorption

During the early Ember Age, guild courier networks, protected trade roads, and city-funded hostels absorbed many Lantern routes.

Some Pilgrims entered the new systems. Some withdrew to roads the guilds found unprofitable. Others became local guest-right orders with no central connection.

No date marks universal dissolution.

The institution ceased to function as one recognizable transregional network while its practices entered:

- courier law;
- neutral rest sites;
- expedition oath;
- road warning;
- hostel peace;
- and protected passage for teachers and healers.

Modern groups lighting three lanterns should be classified as continuations only where transmission, obligation, and reciprocal recognition can be demonstrated.

Most are revivals or cultural heirs.

---

## IX. Ministry Conclusions

1. The Pilgrims were a distributed interpeoples institution, not a church or state.
2. Their three duties were Passage, Provision, and Peace.
3. Yarrow the Kind may represent one founder, several founders, or an institutional title.
4. Lantern Peace created temporary safety without erasing outside law.
5. Guest tokens were limited records before later authorities turned some into access requirements.
6. Beastfolk communities were builders and keepers of the network, not merely its guests.
7. Light-tax funding expanded service while exposing sanctuary to surveillance.
8. The network was absorbed unevenly; later practice is cultural inheritance rather than automatically direct succession.
