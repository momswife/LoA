## **I. Purpose and Scope**

This archive records historical actors whose influence cannot be understood only through the civilizations or events around them.

It contains three related classes of subject:

1. **Figures** — people whose choices, offices, legends, or contested memory changed later history.
2. **Institutions** — organized bodies capable of preserving authority, knowledge, obligation, or practice beyond one generation.
3. **Houses and lineages** — inherited political, sacred, professional, or coerced identities whose continuity carried material consequences.

The section is principally pre-Labyrinth.

Modern ministries, noble houses, corporations, guilds, military orders, media institutions, and public figures belong in the [[The Living Atlas|Living Atlas]]. A modern body may claim descent from an ancient institution. The claim does not move the modern organization into Annals & Antiquities or prove continuous authority.

This archive asks narrower questions:

- Who was the person beneath the founding legend?
- What work did an institution actually perform?
- Who was permitted to join, leave, inherit, or refuse?
- Which obligations survived after their original purpose disappeared?
- What evidence of continuity exists?
- Who benefits when a name is treated as unbroken?

## **III. Biography under Uncertain Evidence**

Ancient biography is rarely a complete account of one life.

Most surviving figures were preserved because later powers needed them to represent something:

- Maelis became the ruler who heard every grievance;
- Othran became the sovereign who could bear every weight;
- Aurelia became the blood through which all lawful light was said to pass;
- Tharos became both forbidden tyrant and liberating king;
- Edrast became the inevitable architect of empire;
- Cael became the fire from which every guild claimed a spark.

These meanings matter.

They are not the same as evidence.

Each biography therefore distinguishes among:

1. **contemporary or near-contemporary record**;
2. **later institutional history**;
3. **oral, household, or hostile memory**;
4. **material evidence**;
5. **modern political use**;
6. and **claims the Ministry cannot presently verify**.

Contradiction is retained where resolving it would require choosing the version most useful to a later regime.

## **IV. Institutions as Durable Action**

An institution is more than a named gathering.

For filing purposes, it normally possesses:

- a recognized purpose;
- a method of membership or appointment;
- procedures capable of surviving leadership change;
- records, rites, tools, or practices that transmit authority;
- and consequences extending beyond one household or moment.

Survival alone does not prove legitimacy.

The [[The Canal Collegium|Canal Collegium]] remained accountable because downstream communities could challenge the measures upon which they depended. The [[The Faith Courts|Faith Courts]] grew powerful by treating sacred uncertainty as a question only authorized interpreters could decide. The [[The Registry of Souls|Registry of Souls]] converted people into durable administrative entries whose assigned purpose could survive them.

The [[Pilgrims of the Three Lanterns]] built law from hospitality without forming a sovereign state. The [[The Ember Collegia|Ember Collegia]] made technical teaching transregional while reproducing new hierarchies of expertise and ownership.

Institutions should therefore be judged through function, access, review, and consequence—not merely through their founding ideals.

## **V. Houses, Dynasties, and Lineages**

These terms are related but not interchangeable.

### **House**

A household or political body that maintains a shared name, estate, office, obligation, or claim. Membership may involve descent, adoption, oath, marriage, service, or legal incorporation.

### **Dynasty**

A sequence of rulers whose succession is presented as continuity. A dynasty may pass through blood, adoption, marriage, ritual recognition, or deliberate rewriting.

### **Lineage**

A descent or transmission relationship. It may be familial, professional, sacred, magical, commemorative, or imposed by the state.

The last category is essential.

The [[The Living Conductor Lines|Living Conductor Lines]] were lineages because imperial law made bodily aptitude and inherited obligation travel together. Their continuity was not proof of family consent. A coerced inheritance remains historically real without becoming legitimate.

## **VI. Continuity Is a Claim**

Ancient names carry modern value.

A current house may claim descent from the Bell-Born line. A guild may call itself a surviving Collegium. A road fellowship may light three lamps and assert direct succession from Yarrow. A ruler may display a seven-ring emblem and claim the blood of Tharos.

The Ministry distinguishes among:

- **genealogical descent**;
- **institutional succession**;
- **cultural inheritance**;
- **revival**;
- **imitation**;
- and **political appropriation**.

One does not prove the others.

A descendant may inherit no office. A revival may honor a practice without possessing its charter. An institution may retain a seal while abandoning the purpose that once justified it.

No record in this section establishes modern sovereignty merely by documenting ancient connection.

## **IX. Ministry Conclusions**

1. Biography, institution, and lineage require different kinds of evidence.
2. Founding legends often preserve political ideals more reliably than personal detail.
3. Institutions remain historically continuous only where function and transmission can be demonstrated.
4. Genealogical descent does not itself establish institutional or sovereign succession.
5. Adoption, oath, office, and craft can create real lineage beside biological descent.
6. States can also impose lineage by making obligation hereditary.
7. Modern claimants must be distinguished from ancient subjects and from later revivals.
8. No record in this section grants modern authority merely by documenting ancient connection.
