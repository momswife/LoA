# The Dhalraeni Burden Rolls

### _The Distributed Records That Turned Infrastructure, Provision, and Care into Both Shared Obligation and Hereditary Assignment_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Antiquities Restoration Bureau  
**Supporting Offices:** Burden-Roll Commission, Dwarven Compact Archive, Road and Hold Survey  
**Primary Compiler:** Magistrate Talar Shellmark, Ancient Obligation and Public Law  
**Contributing Scholars:** Archivist Yelna Stonevoice, Professor Arav Dhal-Senn, Curator Rook-of-Rings  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Record Class:** _Distributed Civic-Engineering Archive / Inherited Duty Record_  
**Evidence Grade:** _Grade-I Crown-Era Series; Grade-II Early Compact Reconstruction_  
**Primary Evidence:** stone strips, metal leaves, leather and cloth rolls, road marks, hold copies, throne recitations, amendment scars, labor disputes, and imperial conversions  
**Historical Range:** Early Dhalraeni coordination under [[Othran the Unbroken]] through Crown and Imperial administration

---

## I. Archive Character

The **Dhalraeni Burden Rolls** recorded what a hold, road, forge, household, office, or sovereign owed to the structures and people allowing the realm to continue.

They were not merely tax lists.

Early rolls included:

- food reserves;
- road and bridge repair;
- water access;
- winter shelter;
- displaced households;
- forge output and waste;
- military service;
- care of the injured;
- burial responsibility;
- unresolved compact disputes;
- and the capacity available to share a burden.

The records joined engineering load to civic obligation.

Later administrations increasingly treated them as schedules for extraction.

---

## II. Material Forms

“Roll” is a translation of function, not one physical format.

Dhalraeni records appear on:

- flexible metal leaves linked by rings;
- cloth and prepared skin;
- thin stone strips fitted into wall racks;
- road markers;
- forge plates;
- drum patterns;
- and temporary tablets designed for seasonal amendment.

Durable central copies survive better than local and temporary ones.

That survival distorts the archive toward royal perspective.

A stone record of obligation may endure after the cloth record of exemption has decayed. Archaeological abundance can make the heavier institution appear more lawful simply because its preferred medium lasted longer.

Missing local copies receive evidentiary consideration where amendment marks prove they once existed.

---

## III. The Early Burden Schedule

During the Winter of Broken Roads, Othran coordinated seven holds and nine forge enclaves through a temporary schedule of need and capacity.

The earliest reconstructed principle was reciprocal:

- name the burden;
- identify who currently carries it;
- identify who benefits;
- determine who can share it;
- record when the obligation ends or must be reviewed.

A hold with grain owed grain because it could prevent death, not because a permanent superior owned its harvest. A road crew received provision because movement benefited the alliance. Refugees entered the schedule as people needing shelter and people possessing skills, kin, and future contribution—not as a single passive cost.

This balance did not survive every later copy.

---

## IV. Recitation at the Stone Throne

Burden records were recited during succession and major rulings at [[The Stone Throne]].

Spoken obligations interacted with the throne’s Weighting mechanism. Sequence, witness, and omission affected resonance. A claimant was expected to hear what the realm required before exercising authority over it.

Court editors learned to shape the recitation.

They could place royal duties in ceremonial language while rendering household labor numerically precise. They could combine many small debts owed to workers into one abstract infrastructure category. They could omit an injured community and reduce the apparent weight of a disputed policy.

The throne responded to the roll presented.

It could not weigh an obligation the archive refused to name.

---

## V. Local Copies and Amendment

Hold Moots negotiated local rolls and maintained copies separate from the royal archive.

Amendment marks show burden transferred, completed, disputed, refused, or returned for review. Dwarven compact copies often preserve maintenance conditions absent from Crown versions. Road communities recorded weather and actual capacity against quotas issued from the citadel.

Contradictory copies are not necessarily corrupt.

They can represent different positions in a negotiated system.

Royal archivists increasingly declared one central copy authoritative. Local disagreement became petition rather than coequal record.

The shift mirrors Dhalraen’s movement from distributed burden to command descending from the throne.

---

## VI. Hereditary Burden

Over time, duties attached to offices and households became duties attached to descendants.

A family maintaining a pass could be recorded as **road-bearing**. A household tending a furnace became a forge line. Mourners, deep listeners, engineers, carriers, and military suppliers found temporary expertise converted into inherited purpose.

Continuity protected essential knowledge.

It also reduced exit.

Later rolls often preserved the obligation while losing the protection, land, ration, or review right originally exchanged for it. Descendants inherited the line that demanded service and not the adjacent line that limited demand.

This pattern later fed the Binding Concord and imperial hereditary duty registers.

---

## VII. People Outside the Roll

Migrants, refugees, exiles, mobile kinships, foreign workers, and unregistered households could be classified as **Roadless**.

Some local rolls integrated them through sponsorship, adoption, service, or recognized need. Others counted their labor while denying their claims. Beastfolk routes were often entered as transport resources rather than living systems with their own law.

Absence could mean exclusion.

It could also be resistance. Some communities refused entry because registration made seasonal obligation permanent and exposed people to conscription or seizure.

The archive does not treat the Roll as a complete population record.

It records what Dhalraeni authorities made legible and what surviving counter-copies restore.

---

## VIII. Imperial Conversion

Imperial administrators admired the Rolls’ capacity to join person, place, infrastructure, and duty.

They standardized categories, removed local amendment rights, assigned Faith Density, and linked obligation to the [[The Registry of Souls|Registry of Souls]]. The result appeared more uniform and was less reciprocal.

Imperial copies often describe inherited duty as an ancient Dhalraeni principle.

That claim is partly true and materially misleading.

Earlier rolls show review, capacity, ending conditions, and obligations owed back to the bearer. Empire preserved the line assigning labor while treating reciprocal terms as local irregularity.

Administrative descent can be demonstrated without calling it faithful continuation.

---

## IX. Ministry Conclusions

1. The Burden Rolls recorded infrastructure, provision, care, capacity, and reciprocal obligation as well as tax and labor.
2. Their many physical formats create a survival bias toward durable central copies.
3. Early schedules emphasized named need, available capacity, shared benefit, and conditions for review.
4. Throne recitation made the archive an active input to the Weighting mechanism.
5. The throne could not account for burdens omitted from the presented record.
6. Contradictory local copies often preserve negotiation rather than corruption.
7. Essential expertise gradually hardened into hereditary duty.
8. Later rolls frequently preserved demanded service while losing reciprocal protection.
9. Roadless absence reflects exclusion, resistance, or both.
10. Imperial systems inherited the Rolls’ administrative power while narrowing their local review and reciprocity.
