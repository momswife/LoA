# The Seven Rings of Tharos

### _The Missing Regalia Preserved through Objects, Oaths, Suppression, and Claim_

**Filed Division:** Ministry of Delving Operations — Annals & Antiquities  
**Responsible Office:** Relics Registrar  
**Supporting Offices:** Western Suppression Archive, Comparative Peoples Bureau, Cultural Custody Review  
**Primary Compiler:** Curator Rook-of-Rings, Relics Registrar and Provenance Specialist  
**Contributing Scholars:** Researcher Falyn Onarra, Inspector Jao “Greyglass” Vell, Archivist Yelna Stonevoice  
**Current Revision:** 3388 A.D., Age of Spectacle  
**Material Class:** _Dispersed or Unconfirmed Regalia Assemblage_  
**Evidence Grade:** _Grade-II Historical Symbol; Grade-III Surviving Object Attribution_  
**Primary Evidence:** claimant rings, imperial confiscation rolls, oral custody traditions, metallurgical tests, ring imagery, refugee records, shrine deposits, and Labyrinth recurrences  
**Historical Range:** Associated with [[Tharos Ravelmane, the Seven-Ringed King]] in the ninth century; dispersed or transformed after 927 A.D.

---

## I. Record Status

No complete authenticated set of the **Seven Rings of Tharos** is held by the Ministry or documented in any verified public collection.

This record therefore concerns an absent assemblage and the objects attributed to it.

The rings may have been:

- seven separate relics worn by Tharos;
- covenant objects held by different councilors;
- seals associated with seven functions or roads;
- one set assembled only during shared council;
- political metaphors later represented as jewelry;
- or a combination of material regalia and distributed office.

The earliest traditions do not support the neat later claim that each ring possessed one stable magical domain.

Uncertainty is not a gap to be filled by the most marketable set.

---

## II. Earliest Descriptions

Near-period accounts describe rings, circles, bands, closed paths, or “voices carried around the hand.”

Some place all seven on Tharos. Others assign rings to gate keepers, road speakers, refuge hosts, or representatives of participating peoples. Several songs describe a ring being returned to council after a campaign, suggesting custody attached to duty rather than personal treasure.

Later traditions associate seven functions with the assemblage:

- passage;
- provision;
- warning;
- memory;
- judgment;
- refuge;
- and shared defense.

These functions correspond more closely to the [[Tharos Ravelmane, the Seven-Ringed King#IV. The Oath of Many Voices|Oath of Many Voices]] than to conventional royal virtues.

If physical rings existed, they may have enacted a divided covenant whose completion depended upon several custodians.

---

## III. The Night of Open Gates

The last secure historical context for the rings is the fall of the Seven-Ring Kingdom in **927 A.D.**

Accounts disagree.

They say the rings were removed from Tharos one by one, divided among refugees, taken by betrayers, buried beneath the stronghold, sealed with the king, or never gathered that night. No captured inventory survives with a complete set.

The earliest Crown seizure lists name circular regalia without consistent description. Later copies may have been altered to support the claim that Tharos possessed only stolen sacred objects.

Refugee traditions preserve different portions of the story because different groups may have escaped with different objects, functions, and memories.

Absence of a unified provenance is historically expected.

It is not proof that every claim is genuine.

---

## IV. Candidate Objects

Candidate rings fall into several material families.

### Western Mixed-Alloy Bands

Heavy bands incorporating iron, silver, copper, horn ash, and trace auric material. Several date plausibly to the ninth or tenth century. None possesses uninterrupted custody to Tharos.

### Council Seals

Larger rings or circular plates bearing multiple marks. Some may have been worn on cords, fitted to staffs, or used to impress clay and wax.

### Refuge Rings

Household objects passed through sanctuary networks. Their significance lies in documented use after the kingdom’s fall, even where manufacture is later.

### Imperial Evidence Rings

Objects held in confiscation collections under Tharos Interdict labels. Several are authentic western artifacts misclassified as royal regalia. Others are obvious teaching replicas or planted evidence.

### Labyrinth Recurrences

Rings recovered from formations shaped by Tharos’s memory. These may display impossible age, personal recollection, or active effects. They are not automatically ancient originals.

---

## V. Claimed Effects

Attributed effects include:

- opening blocked roads;
- compelling safe passage;
- returning a lost person’s name;
- warning connected households;
- strengthening a defender;
- making shelter difficult to violate;
- and altering probability in the bearer’s favor.

No candidate object has demonstrated all seven.

Several authentic western bands respond strongly to oath, route song, household recognition, or acts of sanctuary. The response usually depends upon multiple participants rather than one wearer.

This pattern is consistent with distributed covenant objects.

It is also consistent with later craft made in conscious imitation of the Oath.

Function alone cannot determine date or identity.

---

## VI. Suppression and Manufactured Uncertainty

The **Tharos Interdict of 1137 A.D.** criminalized seven-ring imagery, associated songs, royal titles, and sovereignty claims.

Imperial officers melted, relabeled, dispersed, or seized ring objects. Families concealed provenance to survive. Some replaced confiscated rings and transferred the old duty to the replacement. Others invented pedigrees after suppression made verification difficult.

The empire damaged the evidence and then used damaged evidence to deny the kingdom had existed.

Modern review must not repeat that method.

A missing chain of custody caused by persecution receives contextual weight.

It does not receive automatic acceptance.

---

## VII. Custody and Claimant Harm

Public identification of a candidate ring can endanger its custodians.

Consequences include theft, coerced sale, political recruitment, ritual pressure, invasive testing, and demands that a household prove or surrender ancestry. For that reason, the Ministry does not publish the location of privately held candidates without consent.

Communal custody can be historically valid even where the object is not one of Tharos’s original rings.

A replacement carried through centuries of sanctuary may possess profound cultural importance.

That importance should not be inflated into false material provenance or dismissed because the metal is later.

An object can be a genuine heir without being an original.

---

## VIII. The Complete-Set Fallacy

Collectors and claimants often assume that gathering seven objects will restore Tharos’s authority.

No secure historical source promises this.

The assumption converts a political relationship into a treasure set. It encourages seizure from communities whose separate custody may have been the point of the tradition.

If the rings represented passage, provision, warning, memory, judgment, refuge, and shared defense, their unity required coordinated obligations—not proximity in one display case.

Seven authentic objects gathered through coercion would contradict the inheritance they were said to carry.

The archive will not assist attempts to assemble a set for coronation or sovereign claim.

---

## IX. Ministry Conclusions

1. The seven-ring tradition is historically secure; the surviving original assemblage is not.
2. Early accounts support material regalia, distributed offices, political metaphor, or some combination.
3. The seven functions align more closely with the Oath of Many Voices than with later relic-hunter domains.
4. No candidate object possesses complete provenance to Tharos.
5. Several candidates are genuinely old western covenant objects even where royal attribution is unproved.
6. Imperial suppression deliberately damaged provenance and dispersed custodians.
7. Labyrinth recurrences are evidence of remembered form, not automatic originals.
8. Later ritual successors can possess authentic cultural significance without ancient manufacture.
9. Public disclosure must account for claimant and custodial harm.
10. Neither one ring nor an assembled seven can establish modern sovereignty.
